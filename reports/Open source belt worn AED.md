# Shrinking the defibrillator onto a responder's belt

A belt-worn, open-source automated external defibrillator (AED, the box that reads a collapsed person's heart rhythm and delivers a shock if the heart is quivering) is physically achievable with parts anyone can buy, but nobody has published one that works, and the hard problems are evidence and safety rather than size. Commercial units already deliver a full 150-joule shock at about 0.7 kg and 0.7 litres (St John Pocket AED, Schiller FRED easyport plus), and the "Rive AED" the owner named is most likely the **Avive Connect** (953 g, FDA-approved 2022); the research found no product called "Rive". The only open design, from the University of Pisa (2017), never fired its shock circuit outside a simulation. The best-proven shock shape (biphasic truncated exponential, defined below) is free of its founding patents, a single pad set with a software "child mode" is already accepted by regulators, and the electronics that dominate size (the storage capacitor, the charger and the high-voltage switches) are all available from distributors, with honest estimates of roughly 350 to 620 cm³ and 420 to 800 g for a 150 J design. What does not yet exist openly is the material that makes a defibrillator trustworthy: real cardiac-arrest heart recordings to validate the shock decision, an open instrument to measure the shock, and a regulated organisation willing to act as manufacturer. The most important strategic finding is that the US regulator approved Avive's adult shock on bench proof that it matched Philips' published waveform, with no new human trial, so this project should copy a proven shock and innovate everywhere else. Nothing in this report describes a device that is safe or ready for use on people or animals; it is design research for a device that has no approval of any kind.

## The commercial floor is 0.7 kg for a full-strength shock

### "Rive" is almost certainly the Avive Connect

A search for a "Rive" AED returned nothing of that name; the nearest matches were Avive and Defibtech's older "ReviveR" brand, which is not a compact device ([FDA PMA P160032 via Innolitics](https://fda.innolitics.com/device/P160032)). The researcher put the Avive identification at about 75% confidence, because "a-VIVE" is an easy voice mis-transcription of "Rive". The project has provisionally adopted that reading (decision D-01), and it should still be confirmed with the owner (question Q-01). The engineering conclusions change little either way, because this report covers both Avive and the other very small unit, the CellAED.

The Avive Connect received US premarket approval P210015 on 31 October 2022 for adults and children over one year ([FDA PMA database](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfpma/pma.cfm?id=P210015)). Its specification page lists **953 g including the pad cartridge, 150 J for adults and 50 J for children, a non-escalating impedance-compensated biphasic shock across 25 to 200 ohms, a rechargeable lithium-ion battery that the user cannot replace, at least 75 shocks per charge, 12 months of standby per charge, IP54 sealing, 0 to 50 °C operation, cellular plus Wi-Fi plus GPS, and a machine-learning rhythm analyser** ([Avive spec page](https://www.avive.com/features/aed-specifications)). Two of Avive's own pages disagree on thickness: the specification page says 143 × 160 × 66 mm, the help centre says 143 × 160 × 52 mm ([Avive help centre](https://help.avive.life/hc/en-us/articles/25764077740571-Technical-Specifications)). That puts the box volume at either 1.51 or 1.19 litres; the notes leave the conflict unresolved. Avive is the only device in the comparison that relies on regular recharging instead of a multi-year primary (non-rechargeable) battery, which is both the main reason it is small and a real operating burden.

### CellAED reached 300 g by giving up energy, and paid for it

The Australian CellAED is the smallest unit found: about **300 g (450 g in its case), 196 × 93 × 17 mm, roughly 0.31 litres**, single-use, capped at 20 shocks, with a pre-charged non-rechargeable battery and a 24-month shelf life ([Ambulance Victoria FIRST trial FAQ v1.3](https://www.ambulance.vic.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-06/AV-FIRST-Clinical-Trial-FAQ.pdf)). Its shock energy is reported three different ways, and the notes do not settle which is right: **about 75 J** in marketing material and the spec sheet (via search snippet; the spec sheet itself returned an access error) ([ProTrainings CellAED whitepaper](https://www.protrainings.uk/blog/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/CellAED®-The-latest-development-in-defibrillation-technology-and-therapy.pdf)), **85 J** in the Ambulance Victoria FAQ, and **"up to 150 J"** reportedly in the instruction manual according to a critic's blog that now returns a "not found" error and is known only from a search snippet (unverified) ([critic blog](https://adam-gent-bi3h.squarespace.com/cellaed)). The manufacturer says it uses separate capacitors for each phase of the shock to deliver current comparable to conventional units, and argues that comparing devices by energy "is not a comparison of effectiveness". No peer-reviewed human efficacy data for the low-energy shock was found, and no FDA approval was found.

The regulatory history is cautionary. The UK regulator (MHRA) told Parliament in October 2024 it was investigating concerns ([Parliamentary written question](https://streetguide.co.uk/parliament/written/1731273/5281)), a field safety notice followed on 25 November 2024 ([gov.uk FSN list](https://www.gov.uk/drug-device-alerts/field-safety-notices-25-november-2024)), and a UK retailer reports the device was withdrawn from the UK at the end of 2024 and that the maker entered voluntary administration at the end of 2025 ([Defib4Life, Jan 2026](https://defib4life.co.uk/blogs/insights/cellaed-going-into-administration-what-does-it-mean-for-portable-aed-owners)); that last claim comes from a retailer with a commercial interest and is **unverified** against any official administrator notice. The CellAED pads cover patients over 10 kg and no separate child mode was identified. The lesson for this project is that getting to 300 g meant cutting energy, shock count and service life, and the result struggled with regulators.

### The pocket units show 150 J fits in 0.7 litres

The St John Pocket AED (model P3, launched in Australia in July 2026) claims **under 0.7 kg including battery and pads, 53 × 86 × 150 mm (about 0.68 litres), 150 J adult and 50 J child from one universal pad set with a "Child" button, a disposable 12 V lithium-manganese-dioxide battery of 18 Wh good for 130 shocks at 150 J, IP65 sealing, a 1.5 m drop rating, operation from −15 to +50 °C, 4G/LTE and Bluetooth, and listed compliance with IEC 60601-2-4 and IEC 60601-1-12** (the standard for equipment used by emergency services) ([St John NSW P3 data sheet](https://stjohnnsw.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/SJAA-P3-Pocket-AED-Data-Sheet_v2_FINAL.pdf)). The same data sheet contradicts itself on pad life (4 years in the highlights, 5 in the specifications). The UK "HEART Pocket", launched in April 2026 at 700 g and aimed explicitly at "mobile responders in the emergency services", is probably the same device ([Police Professional](https://policeprofessional.com/news/st-john-ambulance-launches-compact-defibrillator/)); the original equipment maker is not named. The Schiller FRED easyport plus weighs **780 g** at 46 × 150 × 143 mm, delivers "up to 200 J", switches to child mode with a button using the same electrodes, and offers Wi-Fi ([Schiller brochure](https://www.schiller.ch/schiller_images_pdfs/devices/monitoring_defi_cpr/fred-easyport-plus-und-trainer/2.500781_2025-09_de_fred_easyport_plus_pp_lq.pdf)). A widely quoted 490 g figure for the older non-"plus" FRED easyport could not be confirmed from a primary source.

Mainstream units from Philips, Zoll, Stryker and Defibtech weigh 1.4 to 3 kg. None of the numbers in the table were independently measured; all are manufacturer or distributor claims.

How Avive handles children is itself disputed across the notes. One researcher read Avive's specification page as describing **separate paediatric pads** placed front-and-back. Another found that Avive's FDA approval summary and help article describe **the same pad cartridge for adults and children, with a software child mode and a change to front-and-back placement** ([Avive SSED](https://public4.pagefreezer.com:443/content/FDA/03-02-2023T22:45/https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf21/P210015B.pdf); [Avive help: child pads](https://help.avive.life/article/701253/does-the-avive-connect-aed--require-child-pads-)), and the NFL chart lists Avive among "child mode button" devices. The FDA document is the stronger source, and the project's decision D-05 rests on it, but the disagreement should be closed by reading the summary in full.

For police use specifically, the evidence supports officer-carried AEDs where officers reliably arrive first. Vienna police cut the time to first shock from 12 to 6 minutes (p<0.001) ([PMC7266310](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7266310)), and Miami-Dade cut response from about 8 to under 5 minutes ([EurekAlert, AHA 2002](https://www.eurekalert.org/pub_releases/2002-08/aha-awa080702.php)). Where fire services already arrived quickly, adding police AEDs did not help ([Resuscitation 2005](https://read.qxmd.com/read/15964123/providing-automated-external-defibrillators-to-urban-police-officers-in-addition-to-a-fire-department-rapid-defibrillation-program-is-not-effective)). No study compared belt carry with car carry, and no published police specification for a belt AED was found; the researcher's implied target (≤0.7 kg, ≤0.7 L, IP65, 1.5 m drop, operation down to −15 or −20 °C, universal pads with child mode) is inferred from current products.

## No working open AED exists, but the patent record is a usable textbook

The headline finding from the prior-art search is blunt: **no mature open-source AED exists**. The one serious open design is the University of Pisa's "Open-source Automated External Defibrillator" (OAED), published in *HardwareX* in 2017, with a GitHub repository of KiCad circuit files, firmware and MATLAB scripts ([GitHub repo](https://github.com/CentroEPiaggio/Open-Automated-External-Defibrillator)). It has only four commits, the last in July 2017, and its open issues are unanswered questions and spam. Its README states that "tests are needed for the experimental validation" of the high-voltage board and that the 200 J biphasic shock in about 12 seconds comes from simulation ([README](https://github.com/CentroEPiaggio/Open-Automated-External-Defibrillator/blob/master/README.md)). The researcher graded the shock path **SIMULATION / CONCEPT** and the rhythm detection **CLAIMED** (over 90% sensitivity and about 99% specificity, on database signals only). The design uses a flyback charger with a home-made transformer, an output stage built from pairs of IGBT transistors, three levels of isolation and an ADS1298 heart-signal chip ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/319709576_Open-source_Automated_External_Defibrillator)). Two internal conflicts remain open: the abstract names an LPC1768 processor while the firmware folder is a Cypress PSoC project, and the repository licence file says GPL-3.0 while a directory listing says CC BY-SA 3.0 ([open-source-toolkit](https://amchagas.github.io/open-source-toolkit/post/opensource_automated_external_defibrillator-_hardwarex)). The researcher recommends asking the Pisa authors to confirm the licence before reusing any file.

Everything else found is a student paper design (for example a simulated monophasic circuit from UC San Diego, [UCSD PDF](https://isn.ucsd.edu/courses/beng186b/project/2021/Beckman_Bestmann_Gallardo_Mejia_Pendleton_Automated_external_defibrillator.pdf)) or a closed commercial product such as India's hand-crank SanMitra 1000 HCT ([DBT India](https://dbtindia.gov.in/dbt-press/indian-start-makes-affordable-and-dual-powered-defibrillator-sanmitra-1000hct)). No open design has ever been bench-tested into a standard 50-ohm test resistor with measured energy, let alone tested on an animal. A belt-worn open AED would be first on every count. Open rhythm-analysis code is similarly thin: a handful of unlicensed research repositories, the newest from 2022 ([Joey5460/rhythm_analysis](https://github.com/Joey5460/rhythm_analysis)).

Patents fill the gap. Physio-Control's H-bridge patents (the H-bridge is the four-switch output stage that sends current first one way through the patient, then the other) include full schematics ([US 5,824,017](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/5824017)), and a public teardown of a HeartSine unit shows the same idea in practice: high-voltage thyristors (SCRs, switches that can be turned on but not off by their control pin) plus an IGBT ([EEVblog #909](https://www.eevblog.com/2016/08/11/eevblog-909-heart-defibrillator-teardown/)); the part identifications are the researcher's reading and were **unverified** against datasheets. Texas Instruments has published an AED charger reference design, **PMP20899**, that charges a high-voltage capacitor to 1,500 V from a two-cell lithium-ion pack ([electronicsdatasheets mirror](https://www.electronicsdatasheets.com/manufacturers/texas-instruments/reference-designs/PMP20899)), the closest thing to a ready-made building block.

Patent status shapes freedom to design. The Heartstream/Philips family covering the impedance-compensated biphasic waveform is **expired** (for example US 5,601,612, expired 2013) ([Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5601612A/en)), as is Zoll's original rectilinear patent US 6,096,063 (expired 2016) ([Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6096063)) and the early "rhythm identification during compressions" patent US 7,567,837, which lapsed for unpaid fees in 2021 ([Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/US7567837)). Still in force are Philips' dual-algorithm analyse-during-CPR patent **US 10,561,853, anticipated to run to 2036** ([Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/US10561853)), Zoll's boost-converter rectilinear patent US 8,909,335 (expiry around 2033 by the 20-year rule, **unverified**) ([uspto.report](https://uspto.report/patent/grant/8909335)), and **Avive's portfolio of 30 US patents plus Japanese and European ones**, three of which cover the pad cartridge specifically ([Avive patents page](https://www.avive.com/patents)). Google's status labels are assumptions, not legal conclusions, and the Avive claims were not read. A compact belt design sits squarely in Avive's territory, so a freedom-to-operate review by a patent attorney is a precondition for distributing anything.

The open ventilator rush of 2020 is the closest precedent for what goes wrong. About a hundred teams worked in isolation under "open when finished" models, and a review concluded that "a functioning design is necessary but not sufficient": it must be carried through regulation by a manufacturer ([PMC7195895](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7195895/)). The diabetes OpenAPS project succeeded by publishing a safety reference design early and leaning on certified hardware ([PMC6610599](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6610599)). An AED has no certified hardware to lean on, which makes the open safety documentation and test equipment more important, not less.

## Copy the proven shock: 150 J, biphasic, from a 100 µF capacitor

### Waveform shape matters less than current and timing

A defibrillation waveform is the shape of the current pulse over the few milliseconds of the shock. "Biphasic" means the current flows one way, then reverses; it beats the older one-way (monophasic) shock and is the guideline-preferred class ([AHA 2025 Adult ALS](https://cpr.heart.org/en/resuscitation-science/cpr-and-ecc-guidelines/adult-advanced-life-support)). The three commercial families differ in how they cope with patients whose bodies resist current differently (this resistance is called transthoracic impedance and is measured in ohms). The **biphasic truncated exponential (BTE)** simply lets a charged capacitor drain through the patient and cuts each phase off at a time chosen from the measured impedance. **Rectilinear** waveforms (Zoll) actively hold the current flat. **Pulsed** biphasic (Schiller) chops the pulse into rapid bursts.

No human trial shows one biphasic shape beats another for cardiac arrest, and the American Heart Association says each waveform must be judged on its own evidence ([Philips SMART Biphasic Application Note](https://www.documents.philips.com/doclib/enc/fetch/577774/577795/SMART_Biphasic_Application_Note_(ENG)_2.pdf)). The only head-to-head randomised data come from planned cardioversion of a different arrhythmia (atrial fibrillation), where pulsed biphasic did worse than BTE in one trial (58% vs 84% in sinus rhythm at 4 hours) ([PMC5524016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5524016)) and about the same in another ([PMC8235401](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8235401/)). BTE has the strongest cardiac-arrest record: a blinded trial against monophasic gave an adjusted odds ratio for first-shock success of 4.01 ([PubMed 12867305](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12867305/)), and the Philips 150 J BTE reported 96% first-shock efficacy in the ORCA trial (figures as reported by Philips) ([Philips app note](https://www.documents.philips.com/doclib/enc/fetch/577774/577795/SMART_Biphasic_Application_Note_(ENG)_2.pdf)). The researcher's conclusion is that **BTE from a small capacitor is the simplest and most energy-efficient circuit with a large human evidence base**, because rectilinear shaping burns stored energy in resistors and chopping adds switching loss. Newer ideas (triphasic, ramped, nanosecond pulses, low-energy anti-fibrillation pacing) remain animal or bench science for shocks through the chest; that point is labelled **[background, not re-verified]** in the notes.

Physiologically, current rather than energy restores a normal rhythm. Philips quotes guideline bodies calling energy "a non-physiologic descriptor", and its bench data show a 150 J shock from a 100 microfarad (µF, a unit of capacitor size) capacitor charging to **1,683 V and delivering 21.0 A peak into 80 ohms**, against 18.5 A for a competitor's 200 J shock from a 200 µF capacitor ([Philips app note](https://www.documents.philips.com/doclib/enc/fetch/577774/577795/SMART_Biphasic_Application_Note_(ENG)_2.pdf)). The researcher checked that these figures obey Ohm's law. No guideline or major device has switched to dosing by current, and no trial shows it improves outcomes, so a current-dosed design would be a novel claim and a regulatory risk.

### How much energy, and whether to step up

Guidelines set no minimum biphasic energy; the AHA 2025 algorithm gives "initial dose of 120–200 J" and defers to each maker's validated dose, allowing higher energy for later shocks if the device can ([AHA 2025 ACLS algorithm](https://cpr.heart.org/-/media/CPR-Files/CPR-Guidelines-Files/2025-Algorithms/Algorithm-ACLS-CA-Circular-250620.pdf?sc_lang=en)). The one out-of-hospital trial of fixed versus escalating energy (BIPHASIC, Stiell 2007) found no first-shock difference but better conversion on later shocks with escalation, **36.6% versus 24.7%** among the 106 patients who needed more than one shock ([NCT00212992](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT00212992); [NEJM Journal Watch](https://clinician.nejm.org/EM200704270000001)); the notes record that one summary gives the enrolment as 211 rather than 221. Philips disputes how far this applies, arguing that the trial's low-energy arm was "a low-current design not comparable" to its own 150 J waveform. A Singapore in-hospital trial found no significant difference ([PMC5523096](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5523096)). The researcher's position is that ~150 J from a ~100 µF BTE is the defensible minimum, that going below it leaves the clinically validated envelope, and that the ability to step up once (say to 200 J) is a cheap hedge if the capacitor and charger allow it.

### The capacitor sets the voltage, and the voltage sets the hardware

The physics here is the researcher's own arithmetic, labelled as such in the notes. Stored energy is half the capacitance times the voltage squared, so **100 µF at 1,700 V stores 144 J and at 2,000 V stores 200 J**; the Philips "150 J" at 1,683 V therefore refers to a nominal or compensated figure rather than simple stored energy, which the researcher flags for checking. Below about 60 µF the capacitor drains faster than heart cells can respond (the heart's characteristic response time, called chronaxie, is about 3 ms in a **[background, not re-verified]** model), so the practical floor for a single-capacitor BTE is about 60 to 100 µF, consistent with patent claims covering 60 to 150 µF ([US6405081](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6405081)). To push at least about 10 A through a high-resistance 175-ohm patient takes at least 1,750 V regardless of capacitor size, so **roughly 1.7 to 2.0 kV is unavoidable** for an adult shock through the chest. That single number drives the size of the switches, the spacing on the circuit board and the safety rules for anyone working on it.

The same logic explains a newer architectural idea. Avive's patents describe charging four or five 600 V capacitors in parallel and switching them into series for the shock, which lets a lower-voltage charger and denser parts do the work at the cost of many more high-voltage switches ([Justia US 11,607,555](https://patents.justia.com/patent/11607555)). Two-shock "double sequential defibrillation" improved survival in refractory cases in the DOSE-VF trial ([PubMed 36342151](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36342151/)) but needs two devices; the single-device version, moving the pads to front-and-back ("vector change"), was not superior for survival but did end the quivering more often in a secondary analysis ([PubMed 38522736](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38522736/)). Pads that support front-and-back placement are therefore worth having.

### One pad set for adults and children is already accepted

International guidance now gives children an initial shock of 2 to 4 J per kilogram (ILCOR 2025, a weak recommendation on very-low-certainty evidence) ([ILCOR PLS 2025 CoSTR](https://www.ilcor.org/uploads/PLS-2025-COSTR-Full-Chapter.pdf)), and the European Resuscitation Council uses 4 J/kg capped at the adult dose, with front-and-back placement for children under 25 kg ([PubMed 41117571](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41117571/)). AEDs give children about 50 J, either through a resistor built into special child pads (an "attenuator", which on an infant wastes about 115 J to deliver about 25 J ([US7062321](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/7062321))) or through a software child mode. Avive proved its 50 J child shock to the FDA in a regulated pig study: **45 of 48 first shocks (94%) restored circulation, and 48 of 48 within three shocks** ([Avive SSED](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf21/P210015B.pdf)). Pig studies also found attenuated child doses gave better outcomes than adult doses ([PMC2724893](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2724893)) and that variation in paediatric settings did not affect safety ([PubMed 35146463](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35146463/)).

By the researcher's arithmetic, a fixed 50 J dose gives 2 to 4 J/kg only between about 12.5 and 25 kg and roughly 10 to 17 J/kg to a 3 to 5 kg infant; this is above the recommended range but tolerated in animal data and clinical experience. A software-selected energy beats a passive attenuator for a new design because it needs no second pad type, can use a lower charge voltage, and can be tuned. Pad area is the other constraint. Patent paraphrases of the defibrillator standard require **at least 50 cm² per adult pad (150 cm² total) and at least 15 cm² per paediatric pad** ([US11547863](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11547863)); the standard's own text was not read. Philips' infant pad is 32 cm² ([Philips HCM3504A](https://acc.philips.co.uk/healthcare/product/HCM3504A/infant-multifunction-defibrillator-pads-aami-iec-pads)). The researcher's sketch is two identical pads of about 55 to 70 cm² each, just above the adult minimum, with front-and-back placement mandatory in child mode so the pads cannot touch on a small chest. The costs are slightly higher impedance and edge current density in adults (which pushes the voltage up and raises burn risk) and a mandatory roll of the child. ILCOR's 2025 basic life support review keeps side-and-front placement as the speed-optimised default for trained adult rescuers, with front-and-back allowed ([ILCOR BLS 2025 CoSTR](https://www.ilcor.org/uploads/BLS-2025-COSTR-Full-Chapter.pdf)).

Pad gel dries out through its packaging, which raises impedance and degrades both the heart-signal reading and the shock ([US7526345](https://patents.google.com/patent/US7526345)). Patents propose a test bridge inside the sealed pack that the device can measure ([US8005552](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8005552)). Pre-connected pads in a sealed cartridge let the self-test check the whole path through the pads, which matters because expired electrodes are the leading cause of non-ready public AEDs (below).

## The intelligence that matters is a reliable shock decision and fewer pauses

### The shock decision must beat a 1997 bar by a wide margin

The software that decides whether to shock (formally a "rhythm recognition detector") is judged by **sensitivity** (how often it correctly calls a shockable rhythm) and **specificity** (how often it correctly withholds a shock). The de facto targets date from a 1997 AHA statement and are reused by the FDA: **sensitivity above 90% for coarse ventricular fibrillation (VF, the chaotic quivering that a shock can stop) and above 75% for rapid ventricular tachycardia (VT), and specificity above 99% for normal rhythm and above 95% for other non-shockable rhythms and for asystole (flat line)** ([Figuera et al., PLOS ONE 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4956226); [Avive SSED](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf21/P210015B.pdf)). The full table with minimum sample counts and lower confidence limits is reproduced in the notes only as **[background, unverified this session]**. Mature commercial algorithms do far better in the field: a conventional Stryker AED reached 99.3% sensitivity and 98.7% specificity in an Amsterdam study ([Resuscitation 2021](https://www.resuscitationjournal.com/article/S0300-9572(21)00009-5/fulltext)), and Avive's neural network scored 98.9% on coarse VF and 100% specificity on normal rhythm, but it was trained and tested on an internal database of **over 23,000 recordings** ([Avive SSED](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf21/P210015B.pdf)).

Classical single-measure detectors all fail these targets on unselected data; the best in a 2005 comparison reached only 71.2% sensitivity ([PMC1283146](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1283146/)). Combining about 30 measures in a machine-learning classifier reached 94.7% sensitivity and 96.5% specificity on real out-of-hospital cardiac arrest data, and that explainable approach is the baseline any neural network must beat ([PMC4956226](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4956226)). Neural networks since 2018 meet the targets on clean signals with 3 to 8 second windows ([PMC6527215](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6527215/)), and embedded versions now exist: one ran a decision in 383 ms on an AED's 200 MHz processor with 512 kB of memory (figures from a search summary, full text blocked) ([JAHA](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/JAHA.122.026974)). A November 2026 preprint, not peer reviewed, describes a cascade of three small networks in which a second network biased toward the opposite answer checks each decision and any disagreement returns "indeterminate"; its 1.45 MiB model is too large for most small microcontrollers ([arXiv 2611.26393](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26393)). That "second opinion or abstain" pattern suits a safety-critical device and pairs naturally with an independent classical detector.

The data problem is the project's biggest software bottleneck. The open databases on PhysioNet are long-term monitoring recordings from a few dozen patients (for example 35 in the Creighton database, under an attribution licence ([PhysioNet CUDB](https://physionet.org/content/cudb/1.0.0/))), not recordings through defibrillator pads during real arrests. Performance on public databases overstates field performance ([PMC4956226](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4956226)), many published papers let segments from the same patient appear in both training and testing, and the AHA database is sold, not free. Every high-quality study of real arrest recordings with chest-compression interference used private data under agreement ([PMC13282725](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13282725/); [PMC8232133](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8232133/)). The closest open sets are 260 prehospital VF segments recorded on a Laerdal Heartstart 3000, licence unconfirmed ([Mendeley](https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/wpr5nzyn2z/1)).

Children need separate validation. Adult algorithms tested on paediatric rhythm libraries in the early 2000s reached 96 to 99% VF sensitivity and 99.5 to 100% specificity ([Cecchin 2001](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/01.cir.103.20.2483); [PubMed 12883506](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12883506/)), but the specific risk is that infant fast heart rhythms can exceed an adult "rapid VT" rate threshold. No paediatric neural-network validation from 2018 to 2026 and no open paediatric arrest dataset were found.

### Analysing during compressions saves the seconds that matter

Pauses before a shock cost lives: in 815 patients, each 5-second increase in the pre-shock pause cut survival by 18% ([PubMed 21690495](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21690495/)). Analysing the rhythm while chest compressions continue removes that pause, and the one prospective commercial evaluation (Stryker's cprINSIGHT) reached a decision during compressions in **70% of analyses** with 96% sensitivity and 98% specificity ([Resuscitation 2021](https://www.resuscitationjournal.com/article/S0300-9572(21)00009-5/fulltext)). A realistic design decides during CPR when confident and otherwise asks for a short pause. The cheapest reference signal for cancelling compression interference is transthoracic impedance, which the pads already measure for the shock. This is also where the live Philips analyse-during-CPR patent (to about 2036) bites, so the feature carries legal as well as technical risk.

### CPR feedback, pulse detection and connectivity: what the evidence supports

Real-time CPR feedback reliably improves rate, depth and compression fraction, but the survival evidence is mixed. The large prehospital trial found no survival effect, and **crews muted the audible feedback in 14% of cases** ([PubMed 21296838](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21296838/)); a single in-hospital trial found a large effect ([J Intensive Care 2019](https://jintensivecare.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s40560-019-0357-5)). ILCOR 2025 keeps a weak recommendation for feedback **only as part of a quality-improvement programme, and suggests against using it in isolation** ([ILCOR BLS 2025 CoSTR](https://www.ilcor.org/uploads/BLS-2025-COSTR-Full-Chapter.pdf)). Compression rate can be measured from pad impedance with an error of 1.7 compressions per minute, accurate enough for AED rate guidance ([PMC7537815](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7537815)), at no hardware cost. Depth needs an extra sensor, and a single accelerometer overestimates depth on a mattress, where the mattress can account for about 30 to 40% of measured travel ([PMC3565146](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3565146)). A metronome fixes rate reliably in manikin and field studies ([PMC10320236](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10320236)), and prompt wording and sequence alone change time to first shock by tens of seconds in simulation ([PMC4475613](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4475613/)); EMTs' median from recognition to shock was 51 seconds ([PubMed 12385600](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12385600/)).

Automatic pulse detection without a manual check is research-grade. Classifiers using the pad heart signal plus impedance reach about 92 to 94% accuracy on past recordings ([PMC7514786](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7514786)), need no new hardware, but have no prospective validation and **no commercial AED offers the feature**. It belongs as "possible return of circulation, check" advice at most. Likewise, the measure that predicts whether a shock will succeed (AMSA, amplitude spectrum area) is strongly associated with success in observational data ([PubMed 24005007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24005007/)), but the only human trial was small, stopped early and showed no benefit ([eBioMedicine 2023](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/ebiom/article/PIIS2352-3964(23)00109-3/fulltext)), which is why decision D-11 records it but never uses it to withhold a shock.

Connectivity mostly helps get an AED to a patient, which does not apply to a unit already on a responder's belt; even there, a Swedish volunteer-dispatch trial showed no significant gain in AED attachment ([PubMed 36449309](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36449309/)). What carries over is readiness reporting, automatic incident logging and data upload for quality review. Readiness is a real problem: about **15 to 21% of public AEDs fail inspection**, with expired electrodes the leading cause ([PMC9232625](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9232625/); [PubMed 35618078](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35618078/)). Manufacturers' recorded data are locked in proprietary formats that must be converted before research use ([Resusc Plus 2024](https://doaj.org/article/55078aab193b479892aad4fc418099bb)), so a documented open event format would be a genuine contribution.

### Self-test must exercise the shock path, not just the logic

An analysis of FDA adverse-event reports linked to deaths found **45% of failures happened during charging and shock delivery**, with battery and power problems (23%) and pads and connectors (24%) the main causes, and some faults escaped self-test and caused unexpected power-off ([Arizona experts, DeLuca 2012](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/analysis-of-automated-external-defibrillator-device-failures-repo/)). Commercial units test daily, partially charge weekly and do a full-energy charge into an internal load monthly ([ZOLL AED 3 manual](https://zoll.com/-/media/product-manuals/aed3/01-en/9650-000751-01-sf_f.ashx)). Compact-unit recalls show the same failure modes: HeartSine's 2012 Class I recall was for battery software that mistook a brief voltage dip for a flat battery and switched the unit off ([FDA recall record](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfRes/res.cfm?id=112662)), and Defibtech recalled units in 2011 because software could cancel a shock ([OHS Online](https://ohsonline.com/Articles/2011/05/03/Defibtech-Recalling-65000-US-AEDs.aspx)); the notes record conflicting unit counts (65,885 versus 42,000), possibly from separate recalls. The design requirement that follows is a self-test that loads the battery as hard as a real charge does and fires full energy into an internal load on a schedule.

### Rust is qualifiable, but the notes disagree on whether the right chip is covered

The shock-decision software is IEC 62304 **Class C** (the medical software class where failure can cause death), which requires unit-level design and verification and a recorded risk assessment for every third-party software component ([ScienceSoft Class C package](https://www.scnsoft.com/files/documents-package-for-iec-62304-safety-class-c.pdf)). Ferrocene, an open-source Rust compiler, was qualified for IEC 62304 Class C in January 2025 ([Ferrous Systems blog](https://ferrous-systems.com/blog/ferrocene-achieves-iec-62304-qualification/)). The two research notes then **disagree** on two points that matter to decision D-08. One note reports that Ferrocene's qualified targets include **Armv7E-M**, the architecture of Cortex-M4 and Cortex-M7 microcontrollers, and that it holds IEC 61508 SIL 3 with a subset of the core library certified to SIL 2 in December 2025 ([Business Wire, Dec 2025](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20251203818102/en); [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2025/12/04/rust_core_library_partly_polished/)). The other found the qualified targets as of early 2025 were only x86-64 Linux, Armv8-A bare metal and QNX, found **no evidence of a qualified Cortex-M target**, and records the safety level as SIL 4 ([Embedded Computing](https://embeddedcomputing.com/technology/software-and-os/ides-application-programming/ferrocene-becomes-first-rust-toolchain-to-achieve-iec-62304-qualification)). The two may simply reflect different dates (early versus late 2025), but neither note verified the current target list, so the question is open. If Cortex-M is not covered, the project would need Ferrous Systems to qualify that target on request or would have to justify an unqualified compiler. No FDA-cleared device publicly documented as running Rust was found. Formal-methods precedents (an FDA reference infusion pump and a pacemaker challenge) suggest the realistic scope is to model and verify the shock state machine, not all the code ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/infusion-pumps/infusion-pump-software-safety-research-fda)).

## The capacitor is half the box, and 2 kV sets every other limit

### Energy storage: film versus camera-flash capacitors

The researcher's arithmetic from Cornell Dubilier's defibrillator catalogue puts commercial film capacitors at about **0.75 to 1.26 J/cm³ at their rated voltage** ([CDE Defibrillator catalog](https://www.cde.com/resources/catalogs/CDEDefibrillator.pdf)), so a 200 J film part alone occupies roughly 280 to 520 cm³ **[CALC]**. The same maker's press material claims "up to 2 J/cc", which the catalogue dimensions do not support; the researcher notes the conflict and suspects the claim applies to newer or custom parts. The alternative is a bank of 330 V camera-flash (photoflash) aluminium electrolytic capacitors: Chemi-Con's largest PH-series part stores about 10.6 J in a 12.5 × 40 mm can, about **2.15 J/cm³, roughly double film** **[CALC]** ([Chemi-Con PH catalog](https://www.chemi-con.co.jp/products/relatedfiles/capacitor/catalog/PH-LL-e.PDF)). Reaching about 2 kV needs seven cans in series per string; four strings would store about 211 J in about 175 cm³ boxed **[CALC]**. The penalties are real: the electrolytic cans are rated only to +65 °C, need "reforming" after long storage (the datasheet's shelf-life test includes a reconditioning step), need balancing across each string, and can recover up to about 10% of their voltage minutes after being discharged (dielectric absorption), which at 2 kV is about 2 J and above hazard thresholds **[CALC]** ([EFCOG BP261](https://efcog.org/wp-content/uploads/Wgs/Safety%20Working%20Group/Best%20Practices/Worker%20Safety%20-%20Health/Electrical%20Safety/BP261%20Using%20Ground%20Sticks%20%28Hooks%29%20to%20Discharge%20Capacitance.pdf)). Film parts from TDK are rated to only +55 °C working ([TDK product brief](https://www.tdk-electronics.tdk.com/download/2887416/9d4efc0f98fe0a32cdc4daff91cff523/film-capacitors-defi-pb.pdf)). Implantable-defibrillator capacitors reach 2 to 10 J/cm³ by patent claims, but they are custom hermetic parts, not stocked by distributors ([US11450486](https://patentimages.storage.googleapis.com/0d/ea/64/909311659b3880/US11450486.pdf)). Ceramic CeraLink parts store less energy per volume than film and are not a bulk option **[CALC]**.

Heat is a direct conflict with the use case. Sun-parked cars averaged 47 °C cabin air and 69 °C on the dashboard after an hour ([ASU news](https://news.asu.edu/20180516-discoveries-asu-study-hot-cars-can-hit-deadly-temperatures-within-one-hour)), and a secondary report puts extremes near 70 °C air and 100 °C dashboard ([Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/amp/story/best-buys%2Fwhy-a-parked-car-hits-70c-in-the-sun-1.500645677)). Both capacitor types and lithium-ion charging limits sit below those figures, so the device needs a storage rating to at least 70 °C, charging inhibited when hot, and explicit instructions not to leave it on a dashboard; Resuscitation Council UK's 2026 briefing makes the same warning ([RCUK briefing](https://www.resus.org.uk/sites/default/files/2026-07/RCUK%20briefing%20AEDs%20in%20vehicles%20_1.pdf)).

### Charger, output switches and battery

Charging about 230 J in 8 seconds needs about 29 W out and 36 W in at an assumed 80% efficiency **[CALC/EST]**, which favours a two- to four-cell battery over a single cell. Analog Devices' LT3750 and LT3751 capacitor-charger controllers are the obvious parts, but every published design and catalogue transformer found targets 300 to 450 V, not 2 kV ([ADI LT3751](https://www.analog.com/en/products/lt3751.html); [Würth reference](https://www.we-online.com/en/components/icref/analog-devices/LT3750A-Flyback)), so a 2 kV charger needs a custom transformer insulated for the full voltage. TI's PMP20899 reaches 1,500 V; the Philips-equivalent design point at 80 ohms is 1,683 V, so even that reference design would need extending (this comparison is the report writer's, drawn from the two cited figures).

Each switch in the output bridge must block the full capacitor voltage, so a 2 kV design needs **3.3 kV-class** devices. Discrete 3.3 kV silicon-carbide MOSFETs are now stocked by distributors, for example Microchip's MSC080SMA330B4N at 3,300 V with a 107 A pulsed rating ([RS listing](https://uk.rs-online.com/web/p/mosfets/0813825)), against a worst-case peak of about 78 to 80 A into a 25-ohm patient **[CALC]**. The pulsed rating's test duration was not found, and a shock phase lasts milliseconds, so a thermal check against the datasheet curve is mandatory before trusting it. Physio-Control's alternative, thyristors in three legs and two 1,200 V IGBTs in series in the fourth, was chosen because high-voltage IGBTs were too weak at the time ([US5824017](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5824017)). The binding constraint turns out to be isolation for the switch drivers: TI's UCC21750 driver is rated for 2,121 V DC working, which is **94% utilised at 2 kV** with no margin **[CALC]** ([TI UCC21750](https://www.ti.com/product/UCC21750)), and Murata's gate-drive power modules are reinforced only to 800 V rms working ([Murata news 2026](https://www.murata.com/en-global/news/power/isolated-gate-drive-power/2026/1116)). No off-the-shelf driver or supply with at least 3 kV working rating and medical isolation was found, which points toward transformer-coupled gate drive as Physio-Control used.

The battery is not the limiting factor. One 200 J charge takes about 0.08 Wh **[CALC]**, so a single high-drain 21700 lithium-ion cell (Molicel P45B, 4.5 Ah, about 70 g) holds energy for well over a hundred charges ([NKON](https://www.nkon.nl/en/rechargeable/molicel-inr21700-p45b-4500mah-45a.html)); retailers disagree on its continuous rating (45 A versus 35 A). Commercial AED packs are 17 to 19 Wh ([Stryker HeartSine data sheet](https://www.stryker.com/content/dam/stryker/ems/products/heartsine-350p/resources/heartsine-350p-360p-connected-data-sheet.pdf)). The weakness is cold: lithium-ion loses power steeply below 0 °C **[BK]**, cold batteries lengthen charge time and can falsely show low battery ([US5897576](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5897576)), while Panasonic's lithium-manganese-dioxide AED cells hold pulse voltage at −20 °C ([Panasonic AED batteries](https://energy.panasonic.com/ap/business/information/articles/aed)). No quantitative cold comparison was found.

### Isolation, spacing and an independent hand on the trigger

The heart-signal input shares the pads with the shock, so it must survive the device's own 2 kV pulse on every use. The usual approach is to disconnect it with high-voltage reed relays during the shock, behind series resistors of tens of kilohms and clamps; Sensata's D-HR relays stand off 10 to 15 kV but carry only 2 to 4 A, so they suit the signal path and **not** the 40 to 80 A shock path ([Sensata/Cynergy3 D-HR datasheet](https://docs.rs-online.com/8c09/0900766b816395c1.pdf)). The standard test for "defibrillation-proof" inputs uses a 5,000 V, 32 µF pulse (about 400 J) and widens clearance to 4.0 mm ([MEDTEQ](https://medteq.squarespace.com/article/category/Testing)). Analog Devices notes that protection parts degrade with repeated shocks ([ADI article](https://www.analog.com/en/resources/technical-articles/lightning-bolts-defibrillators-and-protection-circuitry-save-lives.html)).

Spacing rules at 2 kV are the largest unknown in the hardware. Free sources reproduce the medical standard's creepage and clearance table only up to 566 V DC, where two means of patient protection require 12 mm creepage ([TI SLLA563](https://www.ti.com/document-viewer/lit/html/SLLA563/GUID-BB8ACACE-F248-4744-B32C-5FBCDC2B049F)); the 2 kV values must be read from the purchased standard, and the researcher's guess of 10 to 20 mm is marked **[BK, unverified]**. Secondary sources even disagree on the general-purpose IPC-2221 board spacing increment per volt above 500 V. The practical consequence is that a compact design will rely on solid insulation (inner board layers, barriers, potting) rather than air gaps; whether the pads themselves need full patient-protection isolation from the high-voltage section during the intended shock was **not established**.

Published architectures converge on three independent channels that must all agree before energy reaches the patient: a main processor that requests charge and shock; an independent supervisor (a second small processor or a programmable logic chip, as in the FDA-approved Jewel wearable defibrillator ([FDA SSED P230022B](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf23/P230022B.pdf))) with its own voltage measurement, charge time-out, armed-time limit and "I'm healthy" handshake ([EP0671185B1](https://data.epo.org/publication-server/rest/v1.2/patents/EP0671185NWB1/document.html)); and a hardware interlock such as the shock button wired directly into the switch enable chain ([US6029085](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6029085)). An internal load serves both as the weekly self-test target and as the dump path when anything looks wrong ([US5919212](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5919212)). The researcher stresses that every interlock against an accidental shock is also a new way to fail to shock, and the risk analysis has to balance the two explicitly.

### A realistic size budget

The researcher's own estimate, labelled **[EST]** throughout, puts a 200 J design at roughly **400 to 750 cm³ and 480 to 950 g**, and a 150 J design at about **350 to 620 cm³ and 420 to 800 g**, before pads. The capacitor is 30 to 50% of both volume and mass; capacitor masses were assumed at 1.2 to 1.6 g/cm³ with no source. That lands at about half Avive's volume and in the same range as the St John and Schiller pocket units, but well above CellAED's 300 g, which the researcher judges unreachable at 150 to 200 J with catalogue capacitors.

## Approval runs through a regulated partner and a bench-equivalence argument

In the US, AEDs and their necessary accessories (pads, batteries, paediatric keys) have been Class III devices requiring premarket approval (PMA, the FDA's strictest route) since a 2015 final order, which cited over 40 recalls affecting more than 2 million units ([Federal Register 2015-02049](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2015-02-03/pdf/2015-02049.pdf)). Avive's approval file is the template. Its adult waveform needed **no new clinical study**: Avive captured its voltage and current side by side with the Philips 150 J BTE across simulated patient loads, and the FDA accepted that "the waveforms are almost identical" and relied on Philips' published trials. The child waveform needed a regulated pig study; the rhythm analyser needed a large held-out test set with one record per person; and the file included software hazard analysis to IEC 62304, cybersecurity documentation, a usability study with 70 participants, and a post-approval study ([Avive SSED](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf21/P210015B.pdf)). The FDA fee alone for an original PMA was **$579,272, or $144,818 for a qualifying small business, in fiscal 2026** ([RAPS](https://www.raps.org/resource/fda-unveils-fy-2026-user-fee-rates.html)); fiscal 2027 figures of $636,732 and $159,183 come from a blog that did not clearly label the year. No published total cost for an AED approval programme was found; testing, quality system, animal and usability work dominate.

The EU is arguably harder for a newcomer. Official guidance places automated and semi-automatic defibrillators, with integral pads, in Class III under Rule 22, while a manual defibrillator without built-in diagnosis falls to Class IIb ([MDCG 2021-24 Rev.1](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/cbb19821-a517-4e13-bf87-fdc6ddd1782e_en)); relying on another maker's device for equivalence in the EU generally requires a contract granting access to its technical file **[UNVERIFIED for AEDs; RA CONSULTANT]**. The governing defibrillator standard is IEC 60601-2-4. One note reports that Amendment 2 was published in 2026 as consolidated Edition 3.2 ([IEC webstore](https://webstore.iec.ch/en/publication/26882)); another saw it in a preview listing but recorded its final publication status as not confirmed. A belt unit for emergency services also points to IEC 60601-1-12, whose numeric temperature, drop and sealing limits are behind a paywall ([ISO 59536](https://www.iso.org/standard/59536.html)). The commonly cited energy accuracy (±3 J or ±15%) and charge-time limits (≤15 s with a new battery) are **[UNVERIFIED]** until the standard is bought.

Connectivity has regulatory weight. The FDA's 2025 final guidance on "cyber devices" reads the ability to connect to the internet broadly, "intentionally or unintentionally through any means" ([FR 2025-11669](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-06-27/html/2025-11669.htm)), revised again in February 2026 ([DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/en-pl/insights/publications/2026/02/fda-issues-revised-cybersecurity-premarket-submission-guidance)). Whether a Bluetooth-only unit that syncs through a phone falls inside that reading was not examined and needs a regulatory consultant. The only published AED security research concerns Zoll's fleet-management software, with a critical remote-code-execution flaw fixed in version 2.2 ([NVD CVE-2021-27481](https://nvd.nist.gov/vuln/detail/CVE-2021-27481)).

On law and liability, regulatory duties attach to whoever makes and markets a device, not to publication: the FDA said a ventilator built from published software would still face premarket review ([FDA ventilator FAQ](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/emergency-situations-medical-devices/faqs-ventilators)), and EU commentary says open-source developers avoid device status when the stated purpose is research, training or developing other devices ([Johner Institute](https://blog.johner-institute.com/regulatory-affairs/open-source-software-as-a-medical-device/)). Whether selling kits, boards or assembled units makes the project a "manufacturer" is a **[LAWYER/RA CONSULTANT]** question. Pennsylvania's AED Good Samaritan law protects good-faith acquirers and users, not designers or manufacturers, and ties immunity to maintenance "according to the manufacturer's operational guidelines" ([42 Pa.C.S. § 8331.2](https://www.legis.state.pa.us/WU01/LI/LI/CT/HTM/42/00.083.031.002..HTM)). Open-hardware licences such as CERN-OHL v2 disclaim warranty ([OSI: CERN-OHL-S](https://opensource.org/?p=2172)), but a patient who never agreed to a licence cannot be bound by it **[LAWYER]**. Calling an unapproved device an "AED" or marketing it for patient use risks misbranding; the notes suggest "defibrillator research platform" until a regulated party holds an approval. The open-device precedent that worked is Tidepool Loop: a nonprofit steward took one frozen version of a community design through FDA clearance in January 2023 and licensed it to a manufacturer ([FDA 510(k) K203689](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfPMN/pmn.cfm?ID=K203689)). For a Class III device, the same structure costs an order of magnitude more.

Developer safety is the first gate, not the last. A 200 J, 2 kV capacitor exceeds the NFPA 70E electrical-safety threshold for capacitors (≥400 V and >0.25 J) about **800-fold** **[CALC]** ([SLAC thresholds](https://www-group.slac.stanford.edu/esh/eshmanual/references/electricalReqHazEnergyThresholds.pdf)), and lab practice calls for engineered discharge paths, permanent voltage indication, grounding tools left in place as visual proof of a safe state, and qualified workers ([EFCOG BP261](https://efcog.org/wp-content/uploads/Wgs/Safety%20Working%20Group/Best%20Practices/Worker%20Safety%20-%20Health/Electrical%20Safety/BP261%20Using%20Ground%20Sticks%20%28Hooks%29%20to%20Discharge%20Capacitance.pdf)). A passive bleeder resistor alone cannot drain the capacitor within the commonly cited five minutes without wasting a large share of the stored energy during a normal hold **[CALC]**, which is why the researcher recommends an active, fail-safe dump as the primary means. These figures are inputs to the lab safety policy, not build instructions. Commercial defibrillator analysers measure energy to ±1% into a 50-ohm non-inductive load, with 25 to 200 ohm accessory loads ([Fluke 7000DP](https://www.flukebiomedical.com/sites/default/files/resources/7000dp_gseng0007.pdf)); sources disagree on the Fluke's energy range (0.1 to 360 J versus 0.1 to 600 J). No validated open analyser exists, and one 2021 "open source defibrillation analyzer" paper could not be confirmed to include design files ([AMiner](https://aminer.org/pub/61a8a3c15244ab9dcb6c3768/low-cost-open-source-defibrillation-analyzer-for-educational-purposes)).

## What this means for the project's decisions and plan

The research supports most of the provisional decisions and sharpens several. **D-02 (BTE) and D-03 (150 J adult)** are the strongest-evidenced choices and the only ones that open Avive's bench-equivalence route; the design target should be a parameter-by-parameter match to the published Philips 150 J waveform across 25 to 175 ohms, which in turn fixes the capacitor near 100 µF and the charge voltage near 1.7 to 2 kV. **Q-07 (fixed or escalating)** remains genuinely contested between Stiell's trial and Philips' rebuttal; laying out the board so a 200 J version is possible, as the plan proposes, is the right hedge, at the cost of a capacitor about a third larger. **D-04 (50 J child mode in software) and D-05 (one 55 to 70 cm² pad set, front-and-back in child mode)** match the researcher's sketch and the FDA's acceptance of Avive's approach, but the disagreement over whether Avive actually uses separate child pads must be closed from the full SSED, and the pad-area minimums must be confirmed from the standard itself. **D-06 (semi-automatic)** is consistent with every compact professional unit found; a manual mode (Q-10) would drop the EU class to IIb but adds a readable screen.

**D-07 (rechargeable)** is the biggest size lever, as Avive shows, but it collides with hot vehicle cabins, lithium-ion's poor cold performance, and the HeartSine recall that came from misreading voltage sag; the self-test must load the battery as a real charge does, and charging must be inhibited outside a permitted temperature window. **D-08 (Rust)** depends on the unresolved Ferrocene question; the prudent planning assumption is that a Cortex-M target may need a qualification request, and that every crate becomes a documented third-party component, which rewards a small dependency set. **D-09 (Bluetooth only)** is sound because connectivity's proven value for a carried device is limited to readiness and logging, but whether Bluetooth alone escapes the FDA's broad "cyber device" reading is a consultant question. **D-11 (record AMSA, never act on it)** matches the evidence exactly. **D-10 (nothing above 50 V until a reviewed lab policy exists) and D-12 (no distributed units)** are reinforced by the 800-fold hazard ratio and by the manufacturer-liability analysis.

Three additions follow for the plan. First, the shock-decision software should pair a classical multi-feature detector with a small neural network and abstain ("continue CPR") when they disagree, tested on patient-separated data and reported honestly against the 1997 targets and against field-level performance, while Phase 1 outreach for real arrest recordings (Q-09) starts immediately because it is the critical path. Second, hardware choices should be made with three now-visible constraints in mind: 3.3 kV-class output switches with a verified millisecond thermal rating, gate-drive isolation that off-the-shelf parts barely cover at 2 kV, and capacitors whose temperature ratings fall below car-cabin extremes. Third, the analyse-during-CPR feature and the compact form factor both sit near live patents (Philips to about 2036, Avive's portfolio), so a freedom-to-operate review should come before either is designed in detail, not after.

## Conclusion

The research reframes what "state of the art" means for this project. On size, the frontier has already been reached commercially: a full 150 J shock fits in about 0.7 litres, and the one device that went much smaller did so by cutting energy and then ran into regulators. On the shock itself, the most valuable innovation is restraint, because the FDA has shown it will accept a newcomer whose waveform demonstrably matches a proven one, and an open project cannot afford the human trial that a novel waveform would demand. The real frontier for an open AED lies in the parts no one has published: an independently checkable shock decision with honest validation, an open analyser to measure the output, an open event-data format, and a complete safety and design history that a regulated partner could adopt.

That shifts the project's risk profile away from electronics and toward access and agreements. The two items on the critical path are not components but relationships: an ambulance service or university willing to share real cardiac-arrest recordings, and a patent and regulatory review that maps where Avive's and Philips' live patents leave room to work. The hardware findings add one practical warning that the commercial spec sheets hide: at 2 kV, insulation for the switch drivers, capacitor temperature ratings and the cold behaviour of a rechargeable battery are where a belt-worn design is most likely to fall short, and they deserve attention as early as the capacitor itself.
