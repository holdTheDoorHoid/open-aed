## Sanity checks against the one published bench point

The Philips application note (research notes, `waveforms_energy_pads.md`, Q2 and Q4) says its 150 J shock from a 100 µF capacitor into 80 Ω charges to 1,683 V and gives 21.0 A peak. That is a manufacturer bench claim, not checked by this project.

| At 80 Ω, adult | Philips (published) | This model |
|---|---:|---:|
| Charge voltage | 1,683 V | 1950 V |
| Stored energy (½·C·V²) | 141.6 J | 190.1 J |
| Peak current | 21.0 A | 22.9 A |
| Delivered energy | "150 J" (label) | 150.0 J |
| Phase 1 / phase 2 | not published in our notes | 4.65 / 3.10 ms |

What the difference means:

- **Different scheme.** This design charges to one fixed voltage for every adult patient, set high enough to reach 150 J at the 25 Ω and 175 Ω extremes. At 80 Ω it therefore starts higher than Philips does and cuts the shock shorter. Philips' 1,683 V may be specific to 80 Ω under its own compensation scheme; the notes do not say whether its voltage is fixed.
- **Internal resistance.** 1,683 V / 80 Ω = 21.04 A exactly, so the Philips figure assumes no internal resistance (or ignores it). Ignoring ours, this model's peak would be 24.4 A. With the assumed 5 Ω internal resistance, matching 21.0 A at 80 Ω would need 1785 V.
- **The "150 J" label.** 1,683 V on 100 µF stores only 141.6 J, less than 150 J, so Philips' 150 J cannot be the energy delivered from that charge. It is a nominal or selected-energy label, or the real capacitance differs. The FDA summary (SSED) should settle this. Our 150 J is modelled energy delivered to the patient, which is the stricter meaning.
- **Peak current.** This model gives 22.9 A at 80 Ω, +9% against Philips. Higher peak current is what Philips argues matters most, but no clinical data in the notes says more is better beyond their level.
