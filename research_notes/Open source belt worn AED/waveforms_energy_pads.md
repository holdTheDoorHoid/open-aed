# External defibrillation waveforms, energy dosing and electrode pads (for a belt-worn open-source AED)

Scope note: research run 2026-11-26 with a small tool budget (17 calls). Items marked **[verified this session]** were seen in a fetched or searched source. Items marked **[background, not re-verified]** come from the researcher's prior knowledge of the literature and must be checked before they go into a design document. Evidence grades: RCT / observational / animal / bench / manufacturer claim / guideline.

## Q1. Waveforms: BTE vs rectilinear vs pulsed biphasic vs newer approaches; tilt and phase duration

### Takeaway
Biphasic waveforms as a class beat monophasic (guideline Class 2a). No human RCT shows one biphasic *shape* (BTE, rectilinear, pulsed) is better than another for cardiac-arrest VF. The only head-to-head randomized comparisons are in elective AF cardioversion, and there the BTE arm did as well as or better than pulsed biphasic. For a minimum-size device, **BTE with impedance compensation from a small (~100 µF) capacitor is the best-evidenced choice**. Triphasic, ramp, chopped and nanosecond waveforms remain animal or bench science for transthoracic use.

### Cited Findings
- AHA 2025 adult ALS: biphasic preferred over monophasic (COR 2a). Single shock then immediate CPR (COR 2a, LOE B-NR). [Guideline] — [AHA 2025 Part 9 Adult ALS](https://cpr.heart.org/en/resuscitation-science/cpr-and-ecc-guidelines/adult-advanced-life-support); summary via [EMCrit 2025 ACLS](https://emcrit.org/emcrit/2025-aha-acls-guidelines/) [verified this session, secondary summary]
- AHA advises that "safety and efficacy data related to specific biphasic waveforms must be evaluated on an individual basis." There is no head-to-head human VF data between manufacturers' biphasic waveforms. [Manufacturer document quoting AHA] — [Philips SMART Biphasic Application Note](https://www.documents.philips.com/doclib/enc/fetch/577774/577795/SMART_Biphasic_Application_Note_(ENG)_2.pdf) [verified this session]
- BTE vs monophasic damped sine, OHCA, blinded RCT (van Alem et al., Resuscitation 2003): adjusted OR for first-shock success with BTE 4.01 (95% CI 1.01–10.0). The trial was small with wide CIs. [RCT] — [PubMed 12867305](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12867305/) [verified this session]
- Schneider et al., Circulation 2000 (ORCA; Philips 150 J fixed BTE vs 200–360 J monophasic, OHCA): 96% first-shock efficacy, 76% ROSC, 28% survival to discharge in the biphasic arm (figures as reported by Philips). [RCT; numbers via manufacturer summary] — [Philips app note](https://www.documents.philips.com/doclib/enc/fetch/577774/577795/SMART_Biphasic_Application_Note_(ENG)_2.pdf); original [Circulation 2000;102:1780, doi 10.1161/01.CIR.102.15.1780](https://doi.org/10.1161/01.CIR.102.15.1780) [DOI background, not re-verified]
- Rectilinear biphasic (Zoll): the ORBIT trial (Morrison, Dorian, Long et al.) compared rectilinear biphasic with **monophasic** damped sine in OHCA under ALS. It was not a comparison with BTE. Zoll's whitepaper calls it the largest OHCA biphasic trial [manufacturer framing]. In a non-OHCA ventricular defibrillation RCT, rectilinear biphasic first-shock efficacy was 99% vs 93% for monophasic (p=0.05). [RCT] — [Cochrane reference list CD006762](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD006762.pub2/references); [Zoll high current vs high energy whitepaper](https://zoll.com/-/media/uploadedfiles/public_site/forms/highcurrentvshighenergy_whitepaper_0712_v1.ashx) [verified this session, search snippets only; ORBIT first-shock numbers not retrieved]
- Deakin et al. 2013 compared rectilinear and BTE waveforms, but only in elective AF cardioversion, not cardiac arrest. — [Cochrane references](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD006762.pub2/references) [verified this session, citation only]
- Pulsed biphasic (Schiller Multipulse Biowave) is a BTE envelope chopped into rapid on/off pulses. In an AF cardioversion RCT (Schmidt et al., JAHA 2017), sinus rhythm at 4 h in AF patients was 58% with pulsed biphasic vs 84% with BTE (Physio-Control) (p=0.002, subgroup table). [RCT, cardioversion only] — [PMC5524016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5524016); [table 3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5524016/table/jah32108-tbl-0003) [verified this session, snippet level]
- A 2021 RCT (Diagnostics) comparing Schiller DEFIGARD Touch 7 pulsed biphasic with BTE in AF cardioversion reported similar success rates, with lower delivered energy for the effective pulsed shocks (non-inferiority on cumulative energy). [RCT, cardioversion] — [PMC8235401](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8235401/); [PubMed 34204498](https://www.pubmed.gov/34204498/) [verified this session]
- Pulsed biphasic in OHCA: a 2008 study of 248 VF patients with a Schiller FRED AED found a trend toward benefit at 130 J (86% termination at 5 s, 73% at 30 s) that was not statistically significant. [Observational] — [Bioautomation 2008 via DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/327d04b129b44044957bc3a53fd7b12a) [verified this session, snippet]
- Older BTE data at low energy: one multicentre trial found 130 J truncated biphasic = 200 J monophasic (86% each). Another found 97% first-shock efficacy at 115, 130 and 200 J. A double-blind trial in short-duration VF found 130 J biphasic 83% vs 200 J biphasic 100% and favoured 200 J. [RCTs, mostly induced VF/EP lab] — [PMC5248978](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5248978); [PubMed 8921795](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8921795/) [verified this session, snippets; exact citations of the individual trials not resolved]
- Multiphasic/triphasic truncated exponential waveforms are described in patents (e.g., US5468254, US5601612). These are not clinical evidence. [Patent] — [US5468254](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5468254); [US5601612](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5601612) [verified this session]

### Inferences
- Waveform shape matters less than **delivered current and phase duration**. Clinically proven waveforms differ mainly in how they handle impedance (BTE: duration/tilt adjustment; rectilinear: series resistance switching to hold current flat; pulsed: duty-cycle chopping).
- A rectilinear waveform needs active current regulation, meaning resistive or switched-mode dissipation. That wastes stored energy and adds parts. Chopping (pulsed) adds switching losses and has the weakest comparative data. **BTE from a small capacitor is the simplest and most energy-efficient circuit with a large human evidence base.** This is the natural choice for minimum size.
- Background model [not re-verified]: the Weiss–Lapicque strength-duration relation with cardiac chronaxie ~3 ms (Kroll's "average current" model, PACE 1994; Irnich) predicts an optimal first-phase duration of about 0.58 × (RC + chronaxie) for a capacitive discharge. With C=100 µF and R=75 Ω (τ=7.5 ms), that gives ~6 ms. Commercial BTE first phases fall in the ~3–12 ms range depending on impedance, and second phases are typically shorter (e.g., about 2/3 of phase 1, or a fixed few ms). Treat as a starting design point to validate on a model, not as a citation.

### Gaps
- Exact phase-duration/tilt tables for Philips SMART, Physio/Stryker ADAPTIV, HeartSine SCOPE and Cardiac Science STAR were not retrieved in readable form. The HeartSine TechNote ([link](https://heartsinelive.s3.amazonaws.com/uploads/2025/10/EMEA-HeartSine-SCOPE-Tech-Note-EN-UK.pdf)) and the Philips app note's waveform pages are the places to look. FDA SSEDs for recently approved AEDs (PMA era, post-2015 order) also list waveform parameters.
- Not found in this session: human transthoracic data on ascending-ramp, low-tilt, triphasic, high-frequency or nanosecond-pulse defibrillation, or on low-energy anti-fibrillation pacing (LEAP). Background [not re-verified]: LEAP and multi-pulse low-energy approaches (Fenton et al., Nature 2009; Luther et al., Nature 2011) are ex vivo/animal work aimed at implantable or internal use. Transthoracic application is unproven because chest-wall current spread makes low-energy far-field pacing impractical. Nanosecond pulsed fields (Efimov/Pakhomov groups, rabbit hearts) are bench or animal only.
- Charge-balanced designs: CellAED describes its waveform as "balanced between phases" (see Q3). No independent data found.

## Q2. Current-based dosing, peak/average current, and impedance compensation

### Takeaway
Physiologically, **current, not energy, defibrillates**. AHA and ERC have encouraged moves toward current-based dosing for two decades, but no guideline or major device has switched to current dosing for AEDs, and no RCT shows that current dosing improves outcomes. Adult transthoracic impedance spans roughly 20–230 Ω in device specs (typical 25–180 Ω). Devices measure it with a low-level AC signal through the pads before the shock and/or from the voltage/current at the start of the shock, then adjust duration, tilt, voltage or energy.

### Cited Findings
- Philips: "current (amperes) has been shown to be a better predictor" of shock success than energy. The note quotes AHA ("Energy is a non-physiologic descriptor… Transition to current-based…") and ERC ("manufacturers are encouraged to explore further this move from energy-based to current-based defibrillation"). [Manufacturer document quoting guidelines] — [Philips SMART Biphasic Application Note](https://www.documents.philips.com/doclib/enc/fetch/577774/577795/SMART_Biphasic_Application_Note_(ENG)_2.pdf) [verified this session]
- Philips bench numbers at 80 Ω: Philips 150 J from a 100 µF capacitor charges to 1,683 V and delivers 21.0 A peak. "Another biphasic waveform" at 200 J from a 200 µF capacitor charges to 1,479 V and delivers 18.5 A peak. The note says human impedance typically ranges 25–180 Ω. [Manufacturer bench claim] — [Philips app note](https://www.documents.philips.com/doclib/enc/fetch/577774/577795/SMART_Biphasic_Application_Note_(ENG)_2.pdf) [verified this session]
- In a swine study (Niemann et al., cited by Philips), body-surface peak current tracked the intracardiac voltage gradient better than energy. Philips 150 J gave a higher peak current and gradient than Physio-Control 200 J and Zoll 120 J. [Animal study; manufacturer-selected] — same source [verified this session]
- Singapore in-hospital fixed-vs-escalating RCT (Anantharaman et al.) included a secondary analysis of **peak current vs conversion**. [RCT secondary analysis] — [Open Access Emerg Med 2017, PMC5523096](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5523096); [NCT00429611](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT00429611) [verified this session; the current threshold value itself was not extracted]
- HeartSine SCOPE claims to compensate "all three variables" (voltage, duration, tilt) across a 20–230 Ω operating range. It compares this with Cardiac Science STAR (25–180 Ω) and Philips SMART (25–180 Ω). [Manufacturer claim] — [HeartSine SCOPE TechNote](https://heartsinelive.s3.amazonaws.com/uploads/sites/23/2013/10/0816_H009-020-005-0_Intl_ScopeTechNote_web.pdf); [brochure](https://www.aedbrands.com/heartsine-scope-biphasic-brochure.pdf) [verified this session, snippets]
- White et al. (Resuscitation 2005), using the Philips 150 J fixed protocol: impedance had no bearing on defibrillation, ROSC or survival. White et al. (Crit Care Med 2004): body weight was not related to shock success. [Observational; cited by manufacturer] — [Philips app note](https://www.documents.philips.com/doclib/enc/fetch/577774/577795/SMART_Biphasic_Application_Note_(ENG)_2.pdf) [verified this session]
- Pad drying raises skin-to-pad impedance. Patents propose pad-viability checks by measuring a hydrogel bridge's impedance. [Patent] — [US8005552](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8005552); [US7139615](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/7139615) [verified this session]

### Inferences
- Simple physics with my own arithmetic (I_peak ≈ V/R, ignoring internal resistance): a 100 µF capacitor at 1,700 V gives ~68 A at 25 Ω, ~23 A at 75 Ω and ~9.7 A at 175 Ω. The time constant is 2.5, 7.5 and 17.5 ms respectively. A BTE device therefore lets high-impedance patients get a lower but longer current, and truncates early in low-impedance patients to avoid wasting energy and to protect the myocardium. That is the "impedance compensation" all BTE AEDs do.
- The Philips numbers check out against Ohm's law (1,683 V / 80 Ω = 21.0 A), so the note's bench data is self-consistent.
- A current-dosed design (target a fixed average current over phase 1 and stretch duration to reach it) is physically sensible for a small device. But it would be a novel dosing claim with no clinical-trial backing, a regulatory risk. The conservative path is to copy a proven BTE formulation's voltage/duration-vs-impedance table.
- Background [not re-verified]: Kerber et al. (Circulation 1988) built impedance-based energy adjustment and found monophasic success depended on ~30–40 A peak current. Biphasic adult waveforms succeed with roughly 15–25 A peak in published bench comparisons. Check this before using it as a spec.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed consensus value for "minimum adult biphasic peak or average current" was retrieved. The peak-current analysis of the Singapore RCT (PMC5523096) should be read in full for a threshold.
- How each commercial device measures impedance (frequency of the sense signal, pre-shock vs during-shock) was not retrieved. Background [not re-verified]: AEDs typically inject a small high-frequency (~kHz range) sinusoidal current through the pads continuously, and some, like Philips SMART, also measure during the first part of the shock and adjust in real time.

## Q3. Minimum effective energy; fixed vs escalating; low-energy devices; guideline positions

### Takeaway
Guidelines (AHA 2025, ERC 2025, ILCOR) do not set a minimum biphasic energy. They defer to each manufacturer's validated first-shock dose (typically 120–200 J) and allow escalation if the device can. The only out-of-hospital RCT of fixed vs escalating energy (Stiell 2007) favoured escalating 200–300–360 J for second and later shocks. That result came from one manufacturer's waveform run at lower-than-standard energy. Sub-150 J claims (CellAED ~75 J) are manufacturer-only, with no peer-reviewed clinical efficacy data found.

### Cited Findings
- AHA 2025: if a first shock fails and the device can escalate, higher energy for later shocks may be considered (COR 2b, LOE B-R). If the manufacturer's dose is unknown, use maximum energy (COR 2b, LOE C-LD). The algorithm gives "initial dose of 120–200 J" for biphasic. [Guideline] — [AHA 2025 ACLS cardiac arrest algorithm (PDF)](https://cpr.heart.org/-/media/CPR-Files/CPR-Guidelines-Files/2025-Algorithms/Algorithm-ACLS-CA-Circular-250620.pdf?sc_lang=en); [AHA Part 9](https://cpr.heart.org/en/resuscitation-science/cpr-and-ecc-guidelines/adult-advanced-life-support); [JEMS summary](https://www.jems.com/patient-care/2025-american-heart-association-guidelines-cardiopulmonary-resuscitation-cardiovascular/) [verified this session, via summaries]
- ERC 2025 adult guidance caps the paediatric dose at "the recommended adult energy (120–200 J, manufacturer dependent)", which implies the same adult range. [Guideline, secondary summary] — [Don't Forget the Bubbles, RC(UK) 2025 PLS summary](https://dontforgetthebubbles.com/resus-council-guidance-2025-paediatric-life-support/) [verified this session]
- BIPHASIC trial (Stiell et al., Circulation 2007): 221 OHCA patients randomized to fixed 150–150–150 J vs escalating 200–300–360 J on the same Physio-Control BTE device. First-shock outcomes did not differ significantly. Among 106 patients needing more shocks, conversion was 36.6% (escalating) vs 24.7% (fixed). [RCT] — [NCT00212992](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT00212992); [NEJM Journal Watch](https://clinician.nejm.org/EM200704270000001); original [doi 10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.106.648204](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.106.648204) [DOI background, not re-verified; one search summary gave n=211]
- Expert disagreement: Philips argues the BIPHASIC low-energy arm was "a low-current design not comparable to the Philips high-current 150J waveform". In other words, a 150 J shock from a 200 µF waveform is not equivalent to a 150 J shock from a 100 µF waveform. [Manufacturer argument] — [Philips app note](https://www.documents.philips.com/doclib/enc/fetch/577774/577795/SMART_Biphasic_Application_Note_(ENG)_2.pdf) [verified this session]
- Singapore in-hospital RCT (Anantharaman et al., Open Access Emerg Med 2017; recruited 2005–2008): 150–150–150 J vs 200–300–360 J showed no significant difference in first-shock termination or ROSC. Conversion at repeat 200/300 J shocks was low, rising to 38.95% at 360 J. The authors suggest escalating quickly to 360 J if 150/200 J fails. [RCT] — [PMC5523096](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5523096); [Dovepress](https://www.dovepress.com/a-multicenter-prospective-randomized-study-comparing-the-efficacy-of-e-peer-reviewed-fulltext-article-OAEM) [verified this session]
- A 2022 systematic review of alternative shock strategies (Pocock et al.) found no optimal biphasic first-shock energy. [Systematic review] — [Pocock et al. 2022 (Warwick PDF)](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/sci/med/research/ctu/trials/posed/publications/pocock_et_al_2022_effectiveness_of_alternative_shock_strategies_for_out-of-hospital_cardiac_arrest_a_systematic_review.pdf) [verified this session, snippet]
- US EMS monitor/defibrillators have maximum biphasic energies of 200–360 J, and guidelines allow initial energies of 120–360 J for OHCA. [Observational, EMS data] — [PMC11908921](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11908921/) [verified this session, snippet]
- HeartSine SCOPE: escalating 150–150–200 J, impedance-compensated. Efficacy claims are manufacturer-sourced (developed with the Northern Ireland Bioengineering Centre). [Manufacturer claim] — [HeartSine brochure](https://www.aedbrands.com/heartsine-scope-biphasic-brochure.pdf); [technical concept paper](https://www.aedbrands.com/heartsine-technical-concept-paper.pdf) [verified this session]
- Avive AED (FDA PMA P210015): non-escalating 150 J adult and 50 J paediatric. [FDA SSED device description] — [FDA approval page](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/recently-approved-devices/avive-automated-external-defibrillator-aed-avive-aed-pad-cartridge-avive-aed-training-cartridge); [SSED P210015B](https://public4.pagefreezer.com:443/content/FDA/03-02-2023T22:45/https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf21/P210015B.pdf) [verified this session]
- CellAED: about 75 J delivering "current comparable to conventional units" via a "patented low-energy biphasic waveform… balanced between phases". Weight 450 g with case. [Manufacturer claim only] — [CellAED brochure (ProTrainings PDF)](https://www.protrainings.uk/blog/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/CellAED®-The-latest-development-in-defibrillation-technology-and-therapy.pdf) [verified this session, snippet]

### Inferences
- For a belt-worn device, a defensible minimum is **~150 J delivered from a ~100 µF BTE** (the Philips/Avive design point), with good human OHCA data (Schneider 2000; White 2004/2005). Going below ~150 J leaves the clinically validated envelope. CellAED's ~75 J claim would need its own human data, which was not found.
- The Stiell result means a fixed-150 J-only device will probably convert fewer refractory/recurrent VF episodes than one able to escalate. Whether that holds for a high-current 100 µF waveform is disputed (Philips vs Stiell). Allowing at least one escalation step (e.g., to 200 J, as HeartSine does) is a cheap hedge if the capacitor and charger can reach it. **Recommendation trade-off:** escalation capability costs either a bigger capacitor or a higher charge voltage.

### Gaps
- Not found: peer-reviewed human efficacy data for CellAED (or its regulatory evidence file). Whether CellAED has FDA approval was not verified.
- ILCOR 2025 ALS CoSTR text on energy was not fetched. Only AHA and ERC summaries were seen.
- ERC 2025 adult ALS primary text was not fetched. Background [not re-verified]: ERC 2021 recommended ≥150 J first biphasic shock and considering escalation, and recommended anterolateral pad position as default (see Q5).

## Q4. Capacitor value and voltage; physics of the smallest capacitor

### Takeaway
Commercial BTE AEDs use roughly 60–200 µF. Philips pioneered (and patented) a 100 µF capacitor charged to ~1.7–2 kV. Physio-Control/Stryker-class designs use ~200 µF at lower voltage. Smaller capacitors need higher voltage to deliver the same current. Delivered *energy* drops, but the capacitor's voltage rating, the switch (IGBT/SCR) rating and creepage/clearance distances become the size drivers.

### Cited Findings
- Philips chose "a smaller 100 microfarad (μF) capacitor that used fewer joules to pack the necessary voltage and current punch". It patented small-capacitor external defibrillation. At 80 Ω: 150 J/100 µF → 1,683 V, 21.0 A; 200 J/200 µF → 1,479 V, 18.5 A. [Manufacturer bench] — [Philips app note](https://www.documents.philips.com/doclib/enc/fetch/577774/577795/SMART_Biphasic_Application_Note_(ENG)_2.pdf) [verified this session]
- Patent claims cover a single capacitor of 60–150 µF discharged as a BTE, with a dependent claim of ~100 µF. [Patent] — [US6405081 (damped biphasic circuit)](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6405081) and related; [US5803927 electrotherapy method](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/5803927) [verified this session, search snippet; exact patent number for the 60–150 µF claim not confirmed]
- Patent families exist for lower-voltage energy storage and "solid state" therapy generators (e.g., "Defibrillation circuit with low voltage energy storage", US11794026; US12280265). These point toward boost-on-demand architectures that avoid storing energy at kV. [Patent] — [US11794026](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11794026); [US12280265](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/12280265) [verified this session, titles only]
- "Compact AED with one distal electrode" patents (US11547863, US11433249) and "Compact AED with external ECG" (US11633613) indicate active IP in the small-AED space. [Patent] — [US11547863](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11547863) [verified this session, titles only; assignee not checked]

### Inferences (my own arithmetic; E = ½CV², I_peak ≈ V/R, τ = RC)
- 100 µF at 1,700 V stores 144 J. At 2,000 V it stores 200 J. At 1,683 V (the Philips 80 Ω point) it stores ~142 J, so the "150 J" label refers to delivered/nominal energy under Philips' compensation scheme, not simply ½CV². That is worth clarifying with the SSED.
- 50 µF at 2,450 V stores 150 J. With τ=3.75 ms at 75 Ω, the discharge becomes too short at low impedance and fine at high impedance. Below ~60 µF the RC time constant at typical 50–75 Ω falls under the ~3 ms cardiac chronaxie, so the capacitor drains before the membrane integrates the current, and energy efficiency drops. **That chronaxie argument puts the practical floor for a single-capacitor BTE at ~60–100 µF, consistent with the patent range.**
- Peak current at the high-impedance limit sets the voltage. To give ≥ ~10 A at 175 Ω, you need ≥ 1,750 V regardless of capacitor size. So **≈1.7–2.0 kV is unavoidable for adult transthoracic BTE**. The capacitor value then sets energy and duration.
- Size drivers are kV film capacitor volume (~100 µF/2 kV film capacitors are the largest single component in most AEDs), HV switching (an H-bridge of 4 IGBTs or SCR+IGBT), and charge time from a small battery (e.g., 150 J in ~8 s needs ~20 W average in plus converter losses).

### Gaps
- Not retrieved: published capacitor specs per device (Avive, HeartSine, CellAED). The FDA SSEDs (e.g., [Avive P210015B](https://public4.pagefreezer.com:443/content/FDA/03-02-2023T22:45/https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf21/P210015B.pdf)) and the IEC 60601-2-4 energy-accuracy tests are the next places to look.
- No peer-reviewed "minimum capacitor for transthoracic defibrillation" optimization study was found in this session.

## Q5. Double sequential defibrillation (DOSE-VF) and vector change: relevance to one compact device

### Takeaway
DOSE-VF showed survival benefit for DSED, and to a lesser extent vector change (VC), in refractory VF. DSED needs two defibrillators, but **vector change is pad repositioning (anterior-posterior)**, which one device can do. The practical design takeaway is pads and instructions that support AP placement, which also serves the paediatric requirement.

### Cited Findings
- DOSE VF (Cheskes et al., NEJM 2022): cluster-randomized with crossover, 6 Ontario paramedic services, 405 patients, stopped early due to COVID-19. Survival to discharge was greater with DSED and with VC than with standard defibrillation. [RCT] — [ACC trial summary](https://www.acc.org/latest-in-cardiology/clinical-trials/2022/11/05/02/59/dose-vf); [PubMed 36342151](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36342151/); original doi 10.1056/NEJMoa2207304 [DOI background, not re-verified] [verified this session]
- Reported numbers [background, not re-verified]: survival 30.4% DSED, 21.7% VC, 13.3% standard. Check against the NEJM paper.
- 2024 secondary analysis: DSED beat standard for shock-refractory VF (survival 28.6% vs 0%, p=0.041) but not for recurrent VF. VC was not superior for survival or ROSC but was superior for VF termination. [RCT secondary analysis] — [PubMed 38522736](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38522736/) [verified this session]
- Critiques: the early stop may exaggerate effects ([REBEL EM](https://rebelem.com/double-defib/)). An Intensive Care Med commentary favours DSED but calls VC "an appealing alternative" if two devices are not available ([Springer PDF](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s00134-023-06993-1.pdf)). [Expert opinion] [verified this session]
- AP vs anterolateral initial placement (Lupton et al., JAMA Netw Open 2024; Portland registry 2019–2023): ROSC 74% AP vs 50.5% AL, aOR 2.64 (1.50–4.65). No significant difference in survival to discharge or neurological outcome. Placement was at crew discretion, so there is selection bias. [Observational] — [PubMed 39250154](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39250154/); [PMC11385052](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11385052/); doi 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.31673 [verified this session]
- Guideline split on adult pad position (as of the sources seen): ERC recommends anterolateral; AHA accepts AL or AP. [Guideline] — [UMEM pearl](https://em.umaryland.edu/educational_pearls/4991/) [verified this session, secondary]

### Inferences
- DSED's mechanism (two near-simultaneous shocks with different vectors and roughly double energy) cannot be done by one small device without a second electrode pair and roughly double the stored energy. That conflicts with constraint (1).
- A **pad set designed for AP placement on everyone** (adults and children) is the single-device way to capture the VC and AP signal. It also matches the paediatric AP recommendation. The cost is that AP placement on an adult needs the patient rolled, which delays the first shock.

### Gaps
- Whether ILCOR/AHA/ERC 2025 changed adult pad-position recommendations after Lupton 2024 and DOSE-VF was not confirmed from primary text.

## Q6. Paediatric defibrillation: dose, evidence, adult doses in children, attenuators vs modes, placement, rhythm analysis

### Takeaway
ILCOR 2025: weak recommendation for an initial **2–4 J/kg** (very low-certainty evidence). ERC/RC(UK) 2025 uses **4 J/kg**, capped at the adult dose, with later shocks at 4–10 J/kg suggested by some councils. AEDs deliver ~50 J (attenuated or software "child mode") for children under 8 years/25 kg, and an adult AED is acceptable when nothing else is available. AP placement is recommended for small children. Evidence is mostly observational and animal.

### Cited Findings
- ILCOR 2025 PLS CoSTR: weak recommendation, initial 2–4 J/kg for infants and children in VF/pVT (citing Acworth et al. 2024; Scholefield et al. 2025). [Guideline/CoSTR] — [ILCOR PLS 2025 CoSTR full chapter](https://www.ilcor.org/uploads/PLS-2025-COSTR-Full-Chapter.pdf) [verified this session, via search summary]
- ERC 2025 PLS (Resuscitation 2025;215 Suppl 1:110767, doi 10.1016/j.resuscitation.2025.110767): defibrillate at 4 J/kg, not exceeding adult energy (120–200 J). Use AP position in children under 25 kg who can easily be turned. Anterior pad mid-chest just left of the sternum, posterior pad between the scapulae. [Guideline] — [PubMed 41117571](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41117571/); [Don't Forget the Bubbles summary](https://dontforgetthebubbles.com/resus-council-guidance-2025-paediatric-life-support/) [verified this session]
- Red Cross: councils variously use 4 J/kg, 2 J/kg, or 2–4 J/kg initially, with 4–10 J/kg for later shocks. Follow the manufacturer's pad size and selection. Use adult pads in anterolateral position on children ≥8 years. [Guideline] — [ARC defibrillation guideline](https://guidelines.redcross.org/guidelines-database/defibrillation-3/); [ARC 2022 pad size and placement in infants and children](https://guidelines.redcross.org/arc-2022-highlights/pad-size-and-placement-in-infants-and-children/) [verified this session]
- ILCOR found no studies on paediatric pad size for paediatric cardiac arrest/IHCA. [CoSTR] — [ILCOR PLS 2025](https://www.ilcor.org/uploads/PLS-2025-COSTR-Full-Chapter.pdf) [verified this session, via summary]
- Attenuator concept: Jorgenson et al. (Crit Care Med 2002) put energy reduction in disposable paediatric electrodes so unmodified adult AEDs could treat children. A related patent estimates that on an infant the attenuator dissipates ~115 J and delivers ~25 J. [Bench/patent] — [Energy attenuator for paediatric AED (catalogue record)](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_71595769); [US7062321 "defibrillating patients of all ages"](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/7062321) [verified this session]
- Swine: attenuated paediatric doses (50/75/86 J) gave better outcomes than adult 200/300/360 J ([Berg et al., Univ. Arizona record](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/better-outcome-after-pediatric-defibrillation-dosage-than-adult-d/); [PMC2724893](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2724893)). A 2022 porcine study found typical variations in AED paediatric-mode settings did not affect safety or efficacy, and paediatric shocks alone did not cause heart damage ([PubMed 35146463](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35146463/)). [Animal] [verified this session]
- ILCOR's AED-in-children review (Resuscitation 2003;57:237–243): adult doses were chosen for adult safety and efficacy only. Older guidance used 200 J for children and 60/100 J for infants. [Review] — [ILCOR AED children PDF](https://www.ilcor.org/data/ILCOR-AED-children.pdf) [verified this session]
- Zoll markets a paediatric mode cleared for infants under 1 year, defaulting to 50 J and configurable down to 1 J. [Manufacturer claim] — [Zoll "Because children are not little adults"](https://legacy.zoll.com/-/media/public-site/medical-technology/defibrilation/pediatric/because-children-are-not-little-adults.ashx) [verified this session]
- Avive: a software child mode (50 J) for children under 8 years or 55 lb (25 kg), with the same pads and AP placement. Indicated for patients over 1 year. [FDA SSED] — [Avive SSED](https://public4.pagefreezer.com:443/content/FDA/03-02-2023T22:45/https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf21/P210015B.pdf); [Avive help: child pads](https://help.avive.life/article/701253/does-the-avive-connect-aed--require-child-pads-) [verified this session]

### Inferences
- A fixed 50 J paediatric shock covers 2–4 J/kg from about 12.5–25 kg, and gives ~10–17 J/kg to a 3–5 kg infant. Animal data and clinical experience say this is tolerated, but it sits above the 2–4 J/kg recommendation. A **software-selected energy is strictly better than a passive attenuator** for a new design: no extra pad SKU, a lower peak voltage is possible, and dose can be tuned. It is also what lets one pad set serve both groups.
- Low-energy BTE from a 100 µF capacitor at 50 J means ~1,000 V stored. Paediatric impedance is lower (often under 50 Ω in infants with small pads), so peak current stays meaningful.

### Gaps
- Not fetched: AHA 2025 Part 8 Pediatric ALS primary text (energy, pad position), and ILCOR paediatric rhythm-analysis (AED algorithm sensitivity and specificity for paediatric rhythms). Background [not re-verified]: AHA/AAMI performance goals require shockable-rhythm sensitivity >90% (coarse VF) and non-shockable specificity >95%, and paediatric validation studies (e.g., Cecchin 2001; Atkins 2003) showed adult AED algorithms performed acceptably on paediatric rhythms. Paediatric VF tends to have higher frequency and amplitude characteristics, and infant sinus tachycardia up to ~250 bpm must not be called shockable.
- Not found: human RCT evidence on the paediatric dose. The evidence base is observational (e.g., Acworth 2024 systematic review per ILCOR) and animal.

## Q7. Universal pads: products, pad size, area/impedance/burns, gel chemistry, shelf life, standards; designing one small set

### Takeaway
Several AEDs now use **one pad set for adults and children**, switching energy by a key, button or software and changing placement to AP for children: Avive (software child mode, FDA SSED), Philips HeartStart FRx (Infant/Child Key with the same SMART pads), Zoll AED 3 Uni-padz (single pad set, paediatric mode). Standard minimums (per patent paraphrase of IEC 60601-2-4): adult ≥50 cm² per electrode, ≥150 cm² total; paediatric ≥15 cm² per electrode, ≥45 cm² total. A single small pad set is feasible if each pad is at or near the adult 50 cm² minimum and placed AP on infants.

### Cited Findings
- IEC 60601-2-4 electrode area (as paraphrased in patents): adult self-adhesive electrodes need ≥150 cm² total active gel area, each ≥50 cm². Paediatric needs ≥45 cm² total, each ≥15 cm². [Standard, secondary] — [US11547863](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11547863); [US11433249](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11433249) [verified this session, search snippet; standard text not seen]
- Product examples: Philips infant multifunction pad, 32 cm² conductor area, for patients under 10 kg ([Philips HCM3504A](https://acc.philips.co.uk/healthcare/product/HCM3504A/infant-multifunction-defibrillator-pads-aami-iec-pads)). A Zoll-compatible paediatric pad has 48.6 cm² gel area per pad ([Nissha spec](https://hs.nisshamedical.com/globalassets/literature/downloads/healthcare-solutions/defibspecs/6600070h-pediatric-zoll.pdf)). [Manufacturer spec] [verified this session]
- Avive: the same pad cartridge serves adults and children; placement is AL for adults and AP for children. [FDA SSED + manufacturer] — [Avive SSED](https://public4.pagefreezer.com:443/content/FDA/03-02-2023T22:45/https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf21/P210015B.pdf); [Avive help](https://help.avive.life/article/701253/does-the-avive-connect-aed--require-child-pads-) [verified this session]
- Hydrogel shelf life: moisture loss through packaging dries the hydrogel. That weakens adhesion, degrades ECG sensing, raises impedance and makes shocks less efficient. High temperature and low humidity speed it up. Expiration dates come from moisture-loss modelling and are conservative. [Patents] — [US7526345](https://patents.google.com/patent/US7526345); [US8005552](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8005552); [US6874621 "increasing electrode shelf life"](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6874621) [verified this session]
- Pad-condition sensing: proposals include a hydrogel bridge in the sealed package whose impedance is checked by the AED, and environment-sensor-adjusted expiry. [Patent] — [US8005552](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8005552); [US7139615 self-contained pad viability tester](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/7139615) [verified this session]

### Inferences
- **Single-set design sketch:** two identical pads of ~55–70 cm² gel area each (e.g., ~8×8 cm square or 8×9 cm rounded). That meets the adult ≥50 cm² minimum and fits AP on an infant: the anterior pad over the sternum/left chest and the posterior between the scapulae, with no risk of the pads touching. The ~32 cm² Philips infant pad shows the infant chest takes roughly 30–50 cm² anteriorly, so an 8×8 cm pad on a newborn would wrap onto the flank. That is acceptable in AP, where the posterior back is larger.
- Trade-offs: (a) smaller adult pads raise transthoracic impedance and current density at the edges, which raises burn risk and lowers current for a given voltage, so it pushes the charge voltage up. (b) Large pads on infants can bridge in AL, so AP must be mandatory in child mode. (c) The universal set needs device-side energy selection (software/key), not attenuation.
- Gel chemistry (background, not re-verified): most AED pads use a conductive hydrogel over a tin or Ag/AgCl-coated conductor. Ag/AgCl gives better defibrillation-recovery and ECG performance (IEC 60601-2-4 has a post-shock offset-recovery requirement). Tin is cheaper. Shelf life (typically 2–5 years) is limited by hydrogel water loss and by corrosion at the conductor-gel interface.
- A belt-worn device benefits from **pre-connected pads in a sealed cartridge** (Avive, HeartSine Pad-Pak, Philips FRx pre-connected) so that the self-test can check the circuit through the pads.

### Gaps
- Not retrieved: ANSI/AAMI DF80 / IEC 60601-2-4 primary text (electrode area, adhesion, impedance limits, AC small-signal impedance ≤ ~3 kΩ, DC offset, defibrillation recovery). The figures above are from patent paraphrases.
- Not retrieved: Philips FRx Infant/Child Key details (it switches the FRx to 50 J paediatric therapy with the same SMART pads, placed AP) and Zoll Uni-padz/AED 3 details [background, not re-verified]. Defibtech and HeartSine use separate paediatric pad-paks or keys [background, not re-verified].
- Not found: peer-reviewed studies of pad area vs transthoracic impedance vs skin burns from 2018–2026. Background [not re-verified]: Dalzell et al. (1989) and Hummel et al. (1988) showed larger pads lower impedance and raise current. Very small pads raise burn and myocardial injury risk.

## Q8. CPR feedback sensors in pads

### Takeaway
Not researched in depth this session, owing to the tool budget. Accelerometer pucks (Zoll Real CPR Help / CPR-D-padz) and impedance-based feedback (Philips Q-CPR, Avive) are common.

### Cited Findings
- Avive markets real-time CPR coaching. — [Avive features](https://avive.life/features) [verified this session, title only; mechanism not confirmed]

### Inferences
- For a minimum-size device, **transthoracic impedance-based compression detection** needs no extra hardware beyond the impedance-sensing circuit already required for shock compensation. An accelerometer puck adds a component to the pad set.

### Gaps
- Not verified this session [background]: Hostler et al. (BMJ 2011;342:d512) found CPR feedback improved CPR quality but not survival. ## Q9. Emerging: dry/long-shelf-life electrodes, printed electrodes, pre-connected pads

### Takeaway
Little peer-reviewed evidence was found. Activity is mainly in patents: shelf-life extension packaging, in-package viability sensing, environment-adjusted expiry.

### Cited Findings
- Packaging to extend electrode shelf life, and evidence that pads may remain usable past the printed date at room temperature or high humidity. [Patent] — [US6874621](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6874621) [verified this session]
- Environment-sensor-adjusted expiry and hydrogel-bridge impedance testing. [Patent] — [US8005552](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8005552) [verified this session]

### Inferences
- For an open-source device, an **in-cartridge viability test** (measure pad-to-pad impedance through a shorting bridge, or a sacrificial hydrogel sample) plus temperature/humidity logging could justify longer field life than a fixed date. Regulators will still require a labelled expiry.

### Gaps
- Not found: clinical or bench studies of dry, printed or hydrogel-free defibrillation electrodes for transthoracic shock. Dry electrodes have high skin impedance and would raise burn risk. No AED on the market was found using them.
