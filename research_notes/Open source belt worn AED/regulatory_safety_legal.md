## 1. US FDA: classification, PMA, accessories, and what publishing vs. distributing means

### Takeaway
AEDs and their "necessary accessories" (pads, batteries, adapters, pediatric keys) are Class III devices requiring premarket approval (PMA) under 21 CFR 870.5310 since FDA's January/February 2015 final order. The most recent new-entrant precedent, Avive (PMA P210015, approved 31 Oct 2022), shows FDA will accept bench testing plus *leveraged published clinical literature* for an adult waveform that is shown to be near-identical to an already-approved waveform, plus a modest GLP swine study for a new pediatric waveform; a novel adult waveform would need far more. The FDA fee alone for an original PMA was $579,272 (standard) or $144,818 (small business) in FY2026.

### Cited Findings
**Classification and the 2015 final order**
- FDA's final order (published 29 Jan 2015, republished 3 Feb 2015, 80 FR) required PMAs for "AED systems", defined as the AED plus accessories necessary for it to detect/interpret an ECG or deliver a shock: "pad electrodes, batteries, adapters, and hardware keys for pediatric use" ("necessary AED accessories"). — [Federal Register 2015-02049 (govinfo PDF)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2015-02-03/pdf/2015-02049.pdf); [eCFR/LII 21 CFR 870.5310](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/870.5310)
- The order's stated rationale was reliability: "Since the January 2011 Panel meeting, over 40 additional class I or class II recalls have been conducted by AED manufacturers and have impacted over 2 million distributed AEDs," with root causes most commonly "design controls, purchasing controls, and receiving acceptance activities." — [FR 2015-02049](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2015-02-03/pdf/2015-02049.pdf)
- On evidence: FDA said it "is aware of clinical study information that can be leveraged for AEDs from both published studies and clinical data previously submitted to FDA under the 510(k) process", and that many accessories "may need to submit nonclinical performance testing with confirmatory animal studies in order to support independent PMA approval." Accessories may be in the AED's PMA or in their own PMA, "including those manufactured by a third party." — [FR 2015-02049](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2015-02-03/pdf/2015-02049.pdf)
- Enforcement timeline for already-marketed accessories was extended: the order gave 60 months (to 3 Feb 2020); a 2020 FDA policy moved non-enforcement for necessary accessories to 3 Feb 2022. — [Federal Register 2020-23841, AED accessories compliance policy](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2020/10/28/2020-23841/necessary-automated-external-defibrillator-accessories-policy-regarding-compliance-date-immediately). For a new entrant in 2026 the transition is moot: everything needs PMA.
- Product code: Avive's PMA is listed under product code **MKJ** (Automated External Defibrillators, non-wearable). — [FDA Browser (Innolitics) P210015 under MKJ](https://fda.innolitics.com/submissions/CV/subpart-f—cardiovascular-therapeutic-devices/MKJ/P210015). Third-party battery PMAs exist, e.g. P190013 "AED Battery Exchange". — [Innolitics P190013](https://fda.innolitics.com/device/P190013)

**Avive PMA P210015 (the best recent template)**
- Approved 31 Oct 2022 for Avive Solutions (San Francisco); company press release dated 23 Jan 2023. Indicated for patients over 1 year; Child Mode for <8 yr or <55 lb. Restricted device, annual reports, and a **post-approval study** required. — [FDA PMA database P210015](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfpma/pma.cfm?id=P210015); [FDA recently-approved devices page](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/recently-approved-devices/avive-automated-external-defibrillator-aed-avive-aed-pad-cartridge-avive-aed-training-cartridge); [Avive press release](https://www.avive.life/news/avive-solutions-receives-fda-approval-of-handheld-automated-external-defibrillator-aed-with-unique-capabilities)
- SSED (P210015B) contents, read directly from the PDF — [SSED P210015B](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf21/P210015B.pdf):
  - Standards cited: IEC 60601-1, 60601-1-2, 60601-1-6, 60601-2-4, IEC 62366, IEC 62304 (software developed and validated per IEC 62304:2015), IEC 60601-1-11 (home healthcare), ISO 10993-1; pads tested for cytotoxicity (ISO 10993-5:2009) and irritation/sensitization (ISO 10993-10:2010).
  - Software documentation included hazard analysis, software FMEA, requirements, architecture, design spec, traceability, static analysis tools for run-time errors, and **cybersecurity documentation**.
  - Waveform: impedance-compensating biphasic truncated exponential (BTE), non-escalating 150 J adult, 50 J pediatric.
  - **Adult waveform: no new clinical study.** Avive showed side-by-side oscilloscope captures (voltage and current) against the Philips 150 J BTE waveform across a range of simulated patient loads, comparing delivered energy, phase peak current/voltage, phase durations and tilt; FDA accepted that "the waveforms are almost identical" and leveraged published Philips clinical data (Schneider et al.; Poole et al.; White et al.). Avive sponsored none of the clinical studies relied on.
  - **Pediatric waveform: GLP swine study.** Safety phase 8 piglets (10-24 kg, 4 test/4 control), all defibrillated on first 50 J shock, survived 24 h with no device-attributed findings. Effectiveness phase 6 piglets x 8 inductions = 48 first shocks after ~30 s untreated VF: 45/48 (94%) ROSC on first shock, 48/48 within 3 shocks; non-inferiority p=0.0004.
  - **Rhythm recognition detector:** a neural network trained on non-overlapping train/validation/test sets drawn from an internal database of "over 23,000 recordings"; final test one record per person, from defibrillators with representative multifunction electrodes. Results vs AHA goals: coarse VF n=274, 98.9% sensitivity (goal >90%); rapid VT n=101, 99.0% (goal >75%); NSR n=137, 100% specificity (goal >99%); other non-shockable n=288, 99.3% (goal >95%); asystole n=105, 100% (goal >95%). (A further column, 97.9%/97.4%/100%/98.5%/100%, is most likely a lower confidence bound; the header did not extract cleanly.)
  - **Human factors:** summative study with 34 trained life-support professionals and 36 untrained lay users, split between simulated adult and pediatric patients, plus maintenance and IFU comprehension tasks.
  - Not referred to an advisory panel.

**Cost of the FDA fee**
- FY2026 (1 Oct 2025-30 Sep 2026) original PMA fee: $579,272 standard; $144,818 for a qualified small business. Small-business status must be re-qualified each fiscal year via Form FDA 3602N. — [RAPS, FY2026 user fees](https://www.raps.org/resource/fda-unveils-fy-2026-user-fee-rates.html); [FDA SBD program](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/premarket-submissions-selecting-and-preparing-correct-submission/reduced-medical-device-user-fees-small-business-determination-sbd-program)
- FY2027 rates were published 30 Jul 2026 — [Federal Register 2026-15335](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/07/30/2026-15335/medical-device-user-fee-rates-for-fiscal-year-2027). A blog lists $636,732 standard / $159,183 small business, but did not clearly label the year — [LexisNexis](https://www.lexisnexis.com/community/life-sciences/b/blog/posts/mdufa-fy2027-medical-device-fees). Verify in the FR notice.

**Investigational use and exemptions**
- 21 CFR Part 812 applies to all clinical investigations of devices to determine safety and effectiveness. A significant-risk (SR) study needs an FDA-approved IDE plus IRB; a non-significant-risk study needs only IRB approval and abbreviated requirements. — [21 CFR 812.2 (LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/812.2); [eCFR Part 812](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-H/part-812)
- The custom device exemption (812.2(c)(7), FD&C Act 520(b)) does not apply when the device is used to determine safety or effectiveness for commercial distribution; a university summary of FDA's guidance describes a limit of no more than five units per year of a device type, with annual reporting to FDA. — [LII 812.2](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/812.2); [Univ. of Kentucky summary](https://research.uky.edu/sites/default/files/2025-03/97-summary_of_fda_regulations_on-exemption_from_ide_requirements.pdf)

**Cybersecurity (section 524B)**
- FDA finalized its 524B "cyber device" section in June 2025 (Section VII of the premarket cybersecurity guidance) and read "ability to connect to the internet" broadly ("intentionally or unintentionally through any means"). — [FR 2025-11669](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-06-27/html/2025-11669.htm); [Hogan Lovells](https://www.hoganlovells.com/en/publications/fda-finalizes-cyber-device-select-updates-guidance)
- A revised version issued 3 Feb 2026 supersedes the June 2025 guidance and aligns references with the amended Part 820 (QMSR, which incorporates ISO 13485). — [DLA Piper, Feb 2026](https://www.dlapiper.com/en-pl/insights/publications/2026/02/fda-issues-revised-cybersecurity-premarket-submission-guidance)
- Law firms note FCA (False Claims Act) enforcement exposure tied to cybersecurity representations. — [Morgan Lewis, Nov 2025](https://www.morganlewis.com/blogs/asprescribed/2025/11/from-vulnerability-to-violation-fda-cybersecurity-requirements-for-medical-devices-and-fca-enforcement)

**Publishing designs vs. distributing devices**
- FDA's ventilator FAQ (COVID era) stated that a new design built from published ventilator software would still be "subject to ... premarket review requirements" (could be added to the umbrella EUA): the regulatory obligation attached to the party making/marketing the device, not the publication. — [FDA FAQs on ventilators](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/emergency-situations-medical-devices/faqs-ventilators)
- Johner Institute (EU-focused) notes liability/obligations arise principally when a device is "placed on the market" and that open source developers avoid device status when the intended use is research, training, or developing other devices; even for "open source medical devices", the manufacturer/marketer must meet the legal obligations. — [Johner Institute: open source software as a medical device](https://blog.johner-institute.com/regulatory-affairs/open-source-software-as-a-medical-device/)
- Case law on liability for published (e.g. 3D-print) design files is sparse. — [Nutter McClennen & Fish (2018)](https://www.nutter.com/assets/htmldocuments/Maguire%20Imparato%20article.pdf); [Expert Institute](https://www.expertinstitute.com/resources/insights/3d-printed-medical-devices-where-does-product-liability-law-go-from-here)

### Inferences
- The Avive precedent is the single most important strategic fact for this project: **copying the parameters of an established, clinically validated BTE waveform (e.g. 150 J impedance-compensating, or 120-200 J rectilinear/escalating) and demonstrating bench equivalence across the load range** is the route FDA has accepted to avoid a human defibrillation trial. A novel waveform (e.g. a lower-energy one chosen to shrink capacitors for a belt form factor) would likely trigger animal efficacy studies plus a human clinical study under an SR IDE.
- The rhythm-detection evidence bar is a *large, independent, one-record-per-patient ECG test set* recorded through defibrillator-type pads. An open project's biggest data problem is access to such a database (Avive used >23,000 proprietary recordings). Public databases (e.g. PhysioNet's MIT-BIH, CU ventricular tachyarrhythmia, AHA database (sold)) are a starting point only. **[UNVERIFIED: no source fetched on public AED ECG databases]**
- "Who is the manufacturer" is the operative legal question in both US and EU. A nonprofit that only publishes design files with a research/education intended use is very likely not a manufacturer under FDA's definition, but anyone who builds and supplies units for patient use is. **[LAWYER/RA CONSULTANT]** to confirm, especially if the project sells kits, PCBs or assembled boards.
- A belt-worn AED for professionals is still an AED (MKJ), not a wearable cardioverter-defibrillator (WCD, a different product code worn on the patient). Confirm product code via FDA pre-submission (Q-Sub, free). **[UNVERIFIED code assignment]**
- Total PMA program cost: the FDA fee is a minor part; QMS, testing at an accredited lab, animal study, human factors, software documentation and post-approval study dominate. No published total cost for an AED PMA was found (see Gaps).

## 2. EU MDR, UK, India, WHO

### Takeaway
Under EU MDR 2017/745, AEDs are **Class III under Rule 22** (therapeutic devices whose integrated diagnostic function significantly determines patient management), per the official MDCG 2021-24 Rev.1 guidance, which also names semi-automatic defibrillators and integral pads. Defibrillators *without* integrated diagnosis (manual) fall under Rule 9 as Class IIb. Class III requires a notified body and clinical evaluation.

### Cited Findings
- MDCG 2021-24 Rev.1, Rule 22 examples, Class III: "Automated external defibrillators (AED) including their pads/electrodes if they are an integral part of the defibrillators" and "Semiautomatic external defibrillators". Rule 9 (Class IIb examples) lists "external pacemakers and external defibrillators with no integrated or incorporated diagnostic function". — [MDCG 2021-24 Rev.1 (European Commission PDF)](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/cbb19821-a517-4e13-bf87-fdc6ddd1782e_en); [MDCG 2021-24 landing page](https://health.ec.europa.eu/latest-updates/mdcg-2021-24-guidance-classification-medical-devices-2021-10-04_en)
- Classes IIb and III require notified-body certificates; a 2025-2026 Commission proposal to simplify MDR/IVDR is under Council discussion. — [Council document ST-16919-2025](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-16919-2025-INIT/en/pdf); [Council WK-6502-2026](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/WK-6502-2026-INIT/en/pdf)
- European adoption of IEC 60601-2-4 Amendment 2 was in draft as EN 60601-2-4:2011/oprA2:2025. — [iTeh EN 60601-2-4/prA2](https://standards.iteh.ai/catalog/standards/clc/e796c1e4-0829-4f67-acd4-f76b2df6c20c/en-60601-2-4-2011-pra2-2024)
- WHO: technical specifications exist for 61 device types and there is a WHO priority list for cardiac/stroke/diabetes devices in MeDevIS; searches did not surface a WHO AED technical specification. — [WHO technical specifications for 61 medical devices](https://www.who.int/publications/m/item/who-technical-specifications-for-61-medical-devices); [WHO priority medical devices](https://www.who.int/activities/prioritizing-medical-devices)

### Inferences
- MDR Class III for AEDs means full QMS audit plus technical documentation review by a notified body, and (as a Class III device) likely a clinical evaluation consultation procedure; leveraging equivalence to another manufacturer's device under MDR Art. 61 generally requires a contract granting access to that manufacturer's technical documentation. **[UNVERIFIED for AEDs specifically; RA CONSULTANT]** This makes the EU route arguably *harder* than the US for an equivalence-based newcomer.
- "WHO prequalification" does not appear to exist for AEDs; WHO's role is specifications and priority lists, not approval. **[UNVERIFIED]**

### Gaps
- MDR Common Specifications for AEDs: none found (CS exist mainly for Annex XVI products and some IVDs). Not verified.
- UK MHRA: not researched in this pass. Background: UKCA/CE recognition arrangements continue; post-market surveillance regulations took effect 2025. **[UNVERIFIED]**
- India CDSCO classification of defibrillators: not researched. Background: under MDR 2017 India, defibrillators are generally regarded as Class C/D (high risk) requiring CDSCO manufacturing/import licence. **[UNVERIFIED]**
- Whether MDCG 2021-24 Rev.1 date (the Rev.1 table changes Rule 22) was 2025 or 2026: not confirmed.

## 3. Standards (with editions and dates)

### Takeaway
IEC 60601-2-4 is still **Edition 3** (2010) with Amendment 1 (2018) and **Amendment 2 published in 2026** (consolidated Edition 3.2, dated 2026-09), which mainly aligns it with 60601-1:2005+A1:2012+A2:2020 and 60601-1-2:2014+A1:2020. Avive's PMA shows the standard set FDA expects: 60601-1, -1-2, -1-6, -1-11, -2-4, 62366, 62304, ISO 10993. Exact numeric limits of 60601-2-4 are paywalled and were not verified.

### Cited Findings
- IEC 60601-2-4 consolidated Edition 3.2 = 2010 base + Amd 1:2018 + Amd 2:2026; Amd 2 CDV vote closed 7 Mar 2025; purpose is alignment with 60601-1 A2:2020 and 60601-1-2 A1:2020. — [IEC webstore consolidated version](https://webstore.iec.ch/en/publication/26882); [en-standard.eu Amd2:2026 listing](https://www.en-standard.eu/iec-60601-2-4-2010-amd2-2026-amendment-2-medical-electrical-equipment-part-2-4-particular-requirements-for-the-basic-safety-and-essential-performance-of-cardiac-defibrillators/); [genorma IEC 60601-2-4/AMD2 ED3](https://genorma.com/en/standards/iec-60601-2-4-amd2-ed3/amp); [IECEE: IEC 60601-2-4:2010/AMD1:2018](https://www.iecee.org/certification/iec-standards/iec-60601-2-42010amd12018)
- Amendment 1 (2018) "clarifies the scope as well as provides additional information for AEDs, defibrillator electrodes, audible warnings and cables." — [ANSI/AAMI IEC 60601-2-4 A1:2018 redline preview](https://webstore.ansi.org/preview-pages/AAMI/preview_ANSI+AAMI+IEC+60601-2-4-2010+A1-2018+RED+LINE.pdf)
- Clause map of 60601-2-4 (from the official preview): 201.12 accuracy of controls (energy accuracy), 201.101 charging time, 201.105 recovery of monitor/ECG input after defibrillation, 201.107 rhythm recognition detector, 201.108 defibrillator electrodes; the standard also covers synchronizer, endurance, readiness for use. 201.4.2.101 requires the manufacturer to "address readiness for use in the RISK MANAGEMENT FILE". - Avive's software was validated to IEC 62304:2015 (i.e., 62304:2006+A1:2015). — [Avive SSED](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf21/P210015B.pdf)

## 4. Open source medical device precedents

### Takeaway
No open-source design has reached patients as a high-risk device without a conventional regulated entity standing behind a specific build. The pattern that works: an organization (nonprofit, company, or university) takes on the manufacturer role for one frozen configuration and gets it cleared; the open design upstream stays unregulated (Tidepool Loop, Coventor, Glia).

### Cited Findings
- **Tidepool Loop**: nonprofit Tidepool submitted end of 2020; FDA cleared 23 Jan 2023 via 510(k) K203689 as an "Interoperable Automated Glycemic Controller", described as the first regulatory clearance of an open-source automated insulin dosing algorithm, originating from the patient-led DIY Loop project. Not distributed directly by Tidepool; licensed to Sequel Med Tech (twiist AID system). — [FDA 510(k) K203689](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfPMN/pmn.cfm?ID=K203689); [Tidepool blog](https://Tidepool.org/blog/tidepool-loop-has-received-fda-clearance); [Tidepool Loop provider page](https://www.tidepool.org/providers/tidepool-loop); [PubMed 37051947](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37051947/)
- **COVID ventilators**: FDA umbrella EUA (24 Mar 2020); University of Minnesota's Coventor was the first novel ventilator design under it, with the specification released open source "so other manufacturers globally can begin their regulatory and production processes." The Global Argon Dark Matter collaboration's Mechanical Ventilator Milano received EUA in ~6 weeks but still needed separate approvals in Europe/Canada. HHS COVID device declarations terminate **26 Dec 2026**, ending these EUAs. — [UMN news](https://twin-cities.umn.edu/news-events/fda-authorizes-first-its-kind-low-cost-ventilator-developed-university-minnesota); [U. Houston](https://www.uh.edu/nsm/physics/news-events/stories/2020/0507-open-source-ventilator.php); [FDA ventilator EUAs page](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/covid-19-emergency-use-authorizations-medical-devices/ventilators-and-ventilator-accessories-euas); [FDA ventilator FAQ](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/emergency-situations-medical-devices/faqs-ventilators)
- **Glia**: stethoscope manufactured in Canada and Gaza under a Health Canada Medical Device Establishment Licence (#6823) for Class I devices; tourniquet licence status not confirmed (OSHWA 2026 notes no industry standard for tourniquets and a pending clinical trial). A Glia member (informal forum comment) described CE as in progress and FDA as "a distant goal because it's so expensive." — [Glia stethoscope page](https://store.glia.org/pages/stethoscope); [Glia docs](https://docs.glia.org/docs/device-projects/stethoscope); [OSHWA Healthware Highlights: Glia, Apr 2026](https://oshwa.org/announcements/healthware-highlights-glia/); [Hackaday.io log](https://hackaday.io/event/168701/log/173441)
- **OSI² ONE** (Open Source Imaging Initiative 50 mT MRI): presented Jan 2023, remains a research platform; no CE marking found; project describes ongoing regulatory documentation work. — [letsdatascience (Jul 2026)](https://letsdatascience.com/news/osi2-one-brings-open-source-portable-mri-34fec67a); [Winter et al., NMR in Biomedicine 2023](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/205957/1/NMR%20in%20Biomedicine%20-%202023%20-%20Winter%20-%20Open%E2%80%90source%20magnetic%20resonance%20imaging%20%20Improving%20access%20%20science%20%20and%20education.pdf)
- Industry wariness: a review in IEEE Transactions on Biomedical Engineering (reported by USC, 2025) found liability for malfunctions makes traditional manufacturers wary of open-source devices, and that replication needs quality-system records, regulatory filings and manufacturing procedures, not just CAD/code. — [USC Viterbi, Jun 2025](https://viterbischool.usc.edu/news/2025/06/could-open-source-medical-devices-transform-healthcare/)
- JDRF commissioned a liability analysis (Bowman and Brooke) for interoperable AID systems. — [JDRF/Bowman and Brooke report](https://www.jdrf.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/Interoperable-AID-Liability-Report_Bowman-and-Brooke_Final.pdf)

### Inferences
- The project should treat its **design history file, risk file, verification records and test protocols as first-class open artifacts**, since the USC/IEEE review indicates those, not schematics, are what make an open design transferable to a regulated manufacturer.
- Tidepool is the closest structural model: nonprofit steward did the regulatory work once, then licensed to a manufacturer. For a Class III PMA device the same structure applies but costs are an order of magnitude higher than a 510(k).

## 5. Liability and law (US/Pennsylvania), licensing, naming

### Takeaway
Pennsylvania's AED Good Samaritan statute (42 Pa.C.S. § 8331.2, last amended 2012) immunizes good-faith acquirers/maintainers/users, not manufacturers or designers, and is conditioned on maintenance "according to the manufacturer's operational guidelines" and on trained users. Its AED definition does not mention FDA approval, but whether an unapproved device qualifies is untested and should be assumed unsafe. Open hardware licenses disclaim warranty but cannot remove tort liability for injury.

### Cited Findings
- 42 Pa.C.S. § 8331.2: (a) a person who in good faith acquires and maintains an AED, or uses one in an emergency, is not liable for civil damages, except intentional harm or gross negligence; (b) acquirers must ensure expected users are trained, maintain/test the AED "according to the manufacturer's operational guidelines", instruct users to activate EMS, and provide data to EMS/providers; (c) training per American Red Cross, AHA or other DOH-approved national standards; (d) no immunity for obstructing EMS; (f) defines AED as a portable device using electric shock to restore a stable rhythm, with no reference to FDA approval. ### Inferences
- **Police/paramedic users are not covered the way lay rescuers are**: EMS providers act under their own scope-of-practice and protocol rules, and agencies buy devices that are FDA-approved; an unapproved device would almost certainly violate EMS agency equipment rules and void Good Samaritan/CASA defenses (gross negligence risk). **[LAWYER]**
- Disclaimers in licenses manage contract/warranty claims among licensees, but negligence and strict product liability to an injured third party (the patient) cannot be waived by a license the patient never agreed to. **[LAWYER]** to assess exposure of (a) publishers of design files, (b) kit/PCB sellers, (c) assemblers.
- Licensing for a safety-critical device: CERN-OHL-W or -S keeps improvements flowing back (useful for safety fixes), but a manufacturer partner holding a PMA may resist strong reciprocity on its enclosure/production files; -W lets them combine with proprietary parts. Software: GPLv3/LGPL vs Apache-2.0 has a similar tradeoff; Apache-2.0 has an explicit patent grant. **[Opinion; no source fetched]**
- Naming: "AED" is a generic term, but calling an unapproved device an AED, or marketing it as for patient use, is promotional labeling that can make it an adulterated/misbranded device. Use "defibrillator research platform" or similar until approved. **[LAWYER/RA CONSULTANT]**

## 6. Developer safety, test methods, animal and clinical stages, pathways

### Takeaway
The regulatory evidence chain runs bench (test loads, analyzers, ECG simulators and recorded-rhythm playback for the RRD) → GLP swine VF studies (only for novel waveforms or pediatric dosing) → human data, which can be borrowed from published literature if the waveform is shown to be equivalent. Lab safety and cost-of-pathway figures were not well sourced in this pass.

### Cited Findings
- Avive's evidence chain is the concrete template: bench testing to standards; side-by-side waveform capture into simulated patient loads; GLP porcine VF model (piglets 10-24 kg, ~30 s untreated VF, 8 inductions each, 24 h survival with blood panels and histopathology); RRD validated on a held-out ECG test set; summative human factors (n=70); post-approval study. — [Avive SSED P210015B](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf21/P210015B.pdf)
- FDA anticipated "nonclinical performance testing with confirmatory animal studies" for accessories and leveraging of published clinical data for AEDs. — [FR 2015-02049](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2015-02-03/pdf/2015-02049.pdf)
- 60601-2-4 includes a recovery-after-defibrillation test with a defined arrangement (Figures 201.105/201.107). — [IEC 60601-2-4:2010 sample](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/15855/a965f4e43e3440c883788b944b27a395/IEC-60601-2-4-2010.pdf)

### Inferences
- A realistic staged plan avoiding live subjects for years: (1) design to an existing published waveform; (2) verify with resistive test loads 25-175 ohm and a commercial defibrillator analyzer (e.g. Fluke Impulse-class) that measures energy, peak voltage/current and timing; (3) RRD development on public and licensed ECG databases, plus closed-loop testing by playing recorded rhythms through an ECG simulator into the pads input; (4) only then a GLP swine study at a contract research lab. **[Analyzer/simulator model specifics UNVERIFIED]**
- Pathway ranking for "reaching patients": (a) license the design/DHF to an established AED maker or a well-funded startup that files the PMA (fastest, cheapest for the project); (b) a nonprofit steward pursuing PMA itself (Tidepool model; needs ISO 13485 QMS, likely millions of dollars and multiple years); (c) university partnership for IDE/animal work; (d) remain an education/reference platform with "not for use on humans or animals" labeling. Low-resource markets do not offer a materially easier path: EU MDR is Class III, and LMIC regulators often rely on FDA/CE reference approval. **[cost/time figures not found]**
